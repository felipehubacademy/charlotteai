## Unit A01 — Building the case

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Argument structure — claim + reasons + evidence with numbered or ranked progression
> **Markers**: [denso] [argument]
> **Real-life context**: You build a persuasive case in writing or speech — laying out claims clearly, supporting them with evidence, and ranking them by force.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Strong opener: "_____ raising the minimum wage is well established."
   **Options**: The case for / The case to / A case of / A case in
   **Answer**: The case for
   **Explanation**: "The case for + noun/-ing" — frames the speaker's position. "The case for raising X", "The case against doing Y". Standard rhetorical opener in op-eds, debate, advocacy writing. Equivalent: "The argument for / against".

2. **multiple_choice** — Numbered argument: "_____, it improves living standards. _____, it stimulates demand. _____, it has minimal effect on employment."
   **Options**: First / Second / Finally — In one / In two / In three — Firstly / Secondly / Endly — One / Two / Three
   **Answer**: First / Second / Finally
   **Explanation**: "First / Second / Third / Finally" — classic numbered structure. "Firstly / Secondly" are British; "First / Second" are universal. "Finally" is preferred over "Third" when ranking by importance (you save the strongest for last). C2 style values "First / Second / Finally" especially.

3. **fill_gap** — Argument escalation: "One reason is that the policy saves money. _____ is that it saves lives. _____, it restores public trust."
   **Hint**: "Another is" and "Most importantly"
   **Answer**: Another / Most importantly
   **Explanation**: Cascade pattern: "One reason is..., Another is..., Most importantly...". Each marker ranks the next claim higher. "Most importantly" (or "Above all", "Crucially") signals the climax — typically your strongest point.

4. **fix_error** — "Firstly the policy works. Secondly it's affordable. Lastly people support it."
   **Hint**: Punctuation — commas after enumerative adverbs
   **Answer**: First, the policy works. Second, it is affordable. Finally, people support it.
   **Explanation**: Adverbial sequencers ("First", "Second", "Finally") take a comma when fronted. Also: avoid contractions in formal argument ("it is", not "it's"). "Lastly" exists but "Finally" is the C2 norm for argumentative climax.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The case for the new programme rests on three pillars. First, the pilot study showed measurable gains in literacy across all participating schools. Second, the cost per student remains within budget — well below the threshold for similar interventions. Most importantly, the teachers themselves are asking for it. The evidence is unambiguous: this programme deserves expansion."
   **Question**: What does the speaker consider the strongest reason?
   **Answer**: that the teachers are asking for it (or "teacher demand")
   **Explanation**: Three-stage structure: "First..." (evidence), "Second..." (feasibility), "Most importantly..." (teacher demand — climax). Closing line "The evidence is unambiguous: ..." lands the conclusion with confident summative force.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The case for action is clear."** — opener framing
2. **"First, the evidence is overwhelming."** — numbered start
3. **"Most importantly, lives are at stake."** — climax
4. **"The evidence is unambiguous."** — summative close
5. **"Three points stand out."** — preview structure

## Unit A02 — Anticipating counterargument

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Pre-empting and refuting objections (Some may object that... However... / Critics might argue... but this overlooks... / It would be a mistake to assume...)
> **Markers**: [denso] [argument]
> **Real-life context**: You strengthen your own argument by acknowledging the strongest counterargument and answering it — showing intellectual seriousness.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Pre-empting: "_____ that the policy would be too expensive. _____, the long-term savings are substantial."
   **Options**: Some may object / However / Some objected / Whereas / Some object / Therefore / Some might / Hence
   **Answer**: Some may object / However
   **Explanation**: "Some may object that..." opens a counterargument with appropriate hedge ("may" — acknowledging it could be raised). "However, ..." pivots to the rebuttal. This is the basic refutation move: name + rebut.

2. **multiple_choice** — Stronger refutation: "Critics might argue the data is too sparse — _____."
   **Options**: but this overlooks the consistency across studies / but this is right / and we agree / so we should stop
   **Answer**: but this overlooks the consistency across studies
   **Explanation**: "But this overlooks..." identifies a specific weakness in the counterargument. Stronger than just "however" because it diagnoses why the objection fails. Common pattern: name the objection + diagnose its flaw + reaffirm position.

3. **fill_gap** — Cautioning against assumption: "_____ that the trend will continue indefinitely. Markets, after all, are not linear."
   **Hint**: "It would be a mistake to assume"
   **Answer**: It would be a mistake to assume
   **Explanation**: "It would be a mistake to assume..." — pre-emptive rebuttal of an implicit assumption. Standard C2 argument move. Variants: "It would be premature to conclude", "We should be wary of assuming".

4. **fix_error** — "Some peoples say the policy is too expensive but they are wrong."
   **Hint**: "Some people say... however" is more polished than "some are wrong"
   **Answer**: Some may object that the policy is too expensive. However, this overlooks the long-term savings.
   **Explanation**: "Some peoples" — wrong noun form. "But they are wrong" — bluntly dismissive, weak rhetorically. The polished form acknowledges the objection ("Some may object that...") and refutes specifically ("this overlooks...").

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The author refused easy victories. 'Critics might argue that universal basic income would discourage work — but this overlooks the consistent finding from multiple pilots that hours worked declined only slightly, mostly among parents and students. It would be a mistake to assume the objection is fatal simply because it is loud. The deeper question is not whether some will work less, but whether the gains in wellbeing justify the trade-off.'"
   **Question**: According to the author, what is the deeper question?
   **Answer**: whether the gains in wellbeing justify the trade-off
   **Explanation**: Three refutation moves: "Critics might argue... but this overlooks...", "It would be a mistake to assume...", "The deeper question is...". Each acknowledges objection, diagnoses its flaw, and reframes towards the speaker's terms.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Some may object that the cost is too high."** — opening objection
2. **"Critics might argue... but this overlooks..."** — naming + diagnosing
3. **"It would be a mistake to assume..."** — pre-emptive rebuttal
4. **"This concern, while understandable, is unfounded."** — softening before refuting
5. **"The deeper question is..."** — reframing past the objection

## Unit A03 — Anaphora, antithesis, tricolon

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Classical rhetorical devices — anaphora (repeated openings), antithesis (X vs Y in parallel), tricolon (three-part structure)
> **Markers**: [denso] [rhetoric]
> **Real-life context**: You write speeches, manifestos, op-eds, or arguments that need memorability and rhythm.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Anaphora: "_____ we believe in fairness. _____ we believe in truth. _____ we believe that the two are inseparable."
   **Options**: First / Second / Above all — In our work / In our lives / In our values — We / We / We — One / Two / Three
   **Answer**: We / We / We (or "In our work / In our lives / In our values")
   **Explanation**: Anaphora — repetition of the same word or phrase at the start of successive clauses. Classical rhetorical device. Creates rhythm, builds emphasis, signals conviction. Common in speeches (MLK, Obama, Churchill).

2. **multiple_choice** — Antithesis: "Ask not what your country can do for you — _____."
   **Options**: ask what you can do for your country / ask what they will do for you / ask for help / ask for nothing
   **Answer**: ask what you can do for your country
   **Explanation**: Antithesis — placing opposing ideas in parallel grammatical structure. Kennedy's classic line. Pattern: "Not X — but Y", where X and Y mirror grammatically. Highly memorable; common in inaugural speeches and manifestos.

3. **fill_gap** — Tricolon: "We came, we saw, _____."
   **Hint**: Caesar's third clause
   **Answer**: we conquered
   **Explanation**: Tricolon — a three-part structure with parallel grammar. "Veni, vidi, vici" — Latin original. Used for memorability and rhythm. Modern: "of the people, by the people, for the people" (Lincoln). Three is the rhetorical number — fewer feels incomplete, more feels excessive.

4. **fix_error** — Make tricolon: "He fought injustice, he overcame poverty, his courage was admirable."
   **Hint**: Match grammatical structure — all three parts as subject-verb-object
   **Answer**: He fought injustice, he overcame poverty, he inspired courage. (or: He fought injustice, overcame poverty, and inspired courage.)
   **Explanation**: Tricolon requires parallel structure. The third clause broke parallelism ("his courage was admirable" — different subject). Restoring "he" + verb + object pattern makes the rhythm work. Or compress into one subject across three verbs.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Her closing argument used every classical device. 'We are not asking for charity. We are not asking for permission. We are asking — finally — to be heard. Not after the next crisis. Not after the next election. Now. Tomorrow is too late, and yesterday is gone.' The room rose to its feet before she had finished."
   **Question**: What three rhetorical devices appear?
   **Answer**: anaphora ("We are not asking..."), tricolon ("we are asking... not after... not after... Now"), antithesis ("Tomorrow is too late, and yesterday is gone")
   **Explanation**: Three anaphoras ("We are not asking..."), then tricolon climax ("Not after... Not after... Now."), capped by antithesis ("Tomorrow is too late, and yesterday is gone"). Classical rhetoric concentrated.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"We came, we saw, we conquered."** — tricolon classic
2. **"Of the people, by the people, for the people."** — anaphora + tricolon
3. **"Ask not what X can do for you — ask what you can do for X."** — antithesis
4. **"Not perfect, but progress."** — antithesis short form
5. **"Faster, cheaper, better."** — tricolon punch list

## Unit A04 — Cause-and-effect chains

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Argumentative causal chains (this leads to / which in turn / thereby / as a result / the consequence is / from this it follows that)
> **Markers**: [denso] [argument]
> **Real-life context**: You connect chains of reasoning — A causes B, which causes C, which is why D matters.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Causal chain: "Wages rose, _____ household spending increased, _____ retail sales hit a record."
   **Options**: which led to / which in turn / which led to / which in turn — therefore / therefore — as / as
   **Answer**: which led to / which in turn
   **Explanation**: "X, which led to Y, which in turn caused Z" — embedded causal chain. "Which in turn" links the second causal step to the first. Common pattern in economic and explanatory writing. Each "which" attaches to the preceding noun phrase.

2. **multiple_choice** — Drawing a conclusion: "_____ that the model must be revised."
   **Options**: From this it follows / From this following / This follows / This is following
   **Answer**: From this it follows
   **Explanation**: "From this it follows that..." — formal C2 inference marker. Used when one claim entails another. Stronger than "this suggests"; closer to "this implies necessarily". Common in philosophy, mathematics, formal argument.

3. **fill_gap** — Causal climax: "Resources shrank, talent left, morale collapsed — _____, the project failed."
   **Hint**: "Inevitably" or "As a result" or "Hence"
   **Answer**: as a result (or "inevitably" or "hence")
   **Explanation**: After a cascade of negative causes, a closing marker delivers the consequence. "As a result", "Hence", "Inevitably", "Consequently" — each works. "As a result" is neutral; "Inevitably" claims the outcome was unavoidable; "Hence" is formal.

4. **fix_error** — "A causes B, which causes C, this causes D."
   **Hint**: For a clean chain, use "which in turn causes" not "this causes" mid-chain
   **Answer**: A causes B, which in turn causes C, which in turn causes D.
   **Explanation**: "This causes" mid-chain breaks the embedded structure. Cleaner: keep "which in turn" links. The chain stays grammatically tight and the cumulative causality is more visible.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The analysis traced the cascade clearly. 'The flood damaged the warehouse, which in turn forced the shutdown of three production lines, which in turn delayed shipments to fourteen retailers. From this it follows that the inventory crisis was not a logistical failure but a contingency one — every link held individually, but no link was designed for what happened next.'"
   **Question**: According to the analysis, what kind of failure was the inventory crisis?
   **Answer**: a contingency failure (not a logistical one)
   **Explanation**: Three causal markers tracing the cascade: "which in turn forced...", "which in turn delayed...", "From this it follows that...". Argument-by-causal-chain. The closing inference reframes the diagnosis.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

3. **"From this it follows that..."** — formal inference
4. **"As a result, the model collapsed."** — closing consequence
5. **"The deeper cause is therefore..."** — diagnostic close

## Unit A05 — Strong conclusions

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Conclusion landing — call to action, vision close, stakes-naming ("The stakes could not be higher", "What is at stake is", "If we fail to act, ...", "The choice before us is clear")
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You close arguments memorably — leaving the reader with action, stakes, or a clarifying frame.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Stakes-naming close: "_____ in this debate is not just policy but identity."
   **Options**: What is at stake / What stakes are / In stake / What stakes in
   **Answer**: What is at stake
   **Explanation**: "What is at stake is..." — opens a stakes-naming sentence. Used to elevate from policy detail to underlying value. "What is at stake is not X but Y" — common pattern for reframing.

2. **multiple_choice** — Conditional warning close: "_____ to act now, we will lose the window entirely."
   **Options**: If we fail / Should we fail / Were we to fail / If we will fail
   **Answer**: If we fail (or "Should we fail")
   **Explanation**: "If we fail to + base" — direct conditional close. Or inverted formal: "Should we fail to + base". Both work; the formal inverted version (M01-style) registers more gravity. "If we will fail" is grammatically wrong (no "will" in if-clauses).

3. **fill_gap** — Strong frame: "_____ before us is clear: pay the cost of action now or pay a higher one later."
   **Hint**: "The choice"
   **Answer**: The choice
   **Explanation**: "The choice before us is clear: X or Y" — binary-frame conclusion. Reframes the entire argument as a single decision. Common in op-eds, manifestos, and political speeches. The colon introduces the two options.

4. **fix_error** — "The stakes are not higher anywhere."
   **Hint**: Use idiomatic "The stakes could not be higher"
   **Answer**: The stakes could not be higher. (or: The stakes have never been higher.)
   **Explanation**: "The stakes could not be higher" — fixed idiomatic conclusion phrase. Variant: "The stakes have never been higher". These are climactic phrases — fixed forms, not constructed sentences. Familiarity with the phrase is part of C2.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "She landed her closing without flourish. 'What is at stake is not whether this policy is popular, but whether this generation chooses to leave the next one with options. The stakes could not be higher. The choice before us is clear: take the harder road now, or accept that we have decided, by inaction, to make the harder road our children's.' She sat down. The room sat with her for a long second."
   **Question**: How does the speaker frame the choice?
   **Answer**: take the harder road now, or accept we have made it our children's
   **Explanation**: Three classical conclusion devices: "What is at stake is not X, but Y" (stakes reframe), "The stakes could not be higher" (fixed climactic phrase), "The choice before us is clear: X or Y" (binary frame). Conclusion architecture at C2.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"What is at stake is not X, but Y."** — stakes reframe
2. **"The stakes could not be higher."** — climactic phrase
3. **"The choice before us is clear."** — binary frame
4. **"If we fail to act, we lose the window."** — conditional warning
5. **"Future generations will judge us by this moment."** — historical frame close

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M13, the student should:
- Build numbered cases (The case for X / First, ... / Second, ... / Most importantly, ...)
- Anticipate and refute counterarguments (Some may object / Critics might argue but this overlooks / It would be a mistake to assume)
- Deploy classical rhetorical devices (anaphora, antithesis, tricolon) with parallel structure
- Connect causal chains (which led to / which in turn / from this it follows that / as a result)
- Close with conclusion architecture (stakes-naming / choice frame / conditional warning / historical frame)
- Match rhetorical intensity to argumentative purpose
- Use binary framing ("not X but Y") to clarify stakes

M14 (Linguagem literária) takes rhetorical craft into literary register — figurative language, sentence rhythm, and prose voice.
