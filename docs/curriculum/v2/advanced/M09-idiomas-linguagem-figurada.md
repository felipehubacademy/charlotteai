## Unit A01 — Hit the nail on the head

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: High-frequency conversational idioms (hit the nail on the head, break the ice, read between the lines, the elephant in the room, the tip of the iceberg) | **Tense**: IDIOM
> **Markers**: [denso] [idiom]
> **Real-life context**: You react to ideas, observations, and conversations with idioms that signal genuine engagement.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "When she said the team was burned out, she _____."
   **Options**: hit the nail on the head / hit the nail on the floor / hit it on the head / hit nailed the head
   **Answer**: hit the nail on the head
   **Explanation**: "Hit the nail on the head" = identify exactly the right point or cause. Fixed idiom — no substitutions. The image is of striking a nail squarely, not glancing off. Used to validate someone else's precise observation.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Someone has to address _____ — we can't keep avoiding it."
   **Options**: the elephant in the room / the elephant on the room / the room's elephant / the elephant under the room
   **Answer**: the elephant in the room
   **Explanation**: "The elephant in the room" = a large, obvious problem everyone is ignoring. Fixed prepositional structure "in the room". Used to introduce something that needs saying but hasn't been.

3. **fill_gap** — "She didn't say it directly, but if you _____ the lines, the answer is no."
   **Hint**: "read between"
   **Answer**: read between
   **Explanation**: "Read between the lines" = infer meaning not explicitly stated. Used when someone communicates indirectly and you've decoded the real message. Crucial idiom for navigating polite refusals.

4. **fix_error** — "What we know is just the tip of iceberg — the real problem is much bigger."
   **Hint**: Article needed before "iceberg"
   **Answer**: What we know is just the tip of the iceberg — the real problem is much bigger.
   **Explanation**: "The tip of the iceberg" — definite article "the" is part of the fixed idiom. The image: only 10% of an iceberg shows above water; the rest is submerged. Used to indicate a small visible part of a much larger hidden whole.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The consultant's first report was diplomatic but unmistakable. 'You've hit the nail on the head on customer churn, but that's only the tip of the iceberg. The real elephant in the room is the leadership culture — and reading between the lines of your own employee survey, your people already know it.'"
   **Question**: According to the consultant, what is the bigger issue beneath customer churn?
   **Answer**: the leadership culture (or "the elephant in the room — leadership culture")
   **Explanation**: Four C1 idioms stacked: "hit the nail on the head" (precise diagnosis), "the tip of the iceberg" (visible portion), "the elephant in the room" (avoided real problem), "reading between the lines" (decoding implicit messages). Consulting-grade prose.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"You hit the nail on the head."** — agreement with precise observation
2. **"Let's address the elephant in the room."** — naming the avoided issue
3. **"That's just the tip of the iceberg."** — small visible part
4. **"Try to read between the lines here."** — decode implicit
5. **"We need to break the ice somehow."** — start a difficult conversation

## Unit A02 — Bite the bullet, cold feet

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Body and emotion idioms (bite the bullet, get cold feet, pull someone's leg, cost an arm and a leg, head over heels) | **Tense**: IDIOM
> **Markers**: [denso] [idiom]
> **Real-life context**: You describe decisions, hesitations, and emotional states with idiomatic precision.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "I'm going to _____ and tell her tomorrow."
   **Options**: bite the bullet / bite the bullets / bite a bullet / bite on the bullet
   **Answer**: bite the bullet
   **Explanation**: "Bite the bullet" = force yourself to do something unpleasant or difficult. Fixed singular "the bullet". Origin: 19th-century battlefield surgery without anaesthesia — soldiers literally bit bullets to endure pain. Now used metaphorically for any unwelcome but necessary action.

2. **multiple_choice** — "He got _____ at the altar and called it off."
   **Options**: cold feet / cold foot / cold feets / colds feet
   **Answer**: cold feet
   **Explanation**: "Get cold feet" = lose nerve at the last moment, typically before a big commitment. Always plural "feet". The image is of feet literally going cold from fear, freezing you in place. Common in wedding/career/decision contexts.

3. **fill_gap** — "Stop joking — are you _____ my leg?"
   **Hint**: "pulling"
   **Answer**: pulling
   **Explanation**: "Pull someone's leg" = tease or joke with someone, usually with a deliberately untrue statement they might believe. Used informally to check whether a claim is serious. Origin disputed but likely 19th-century British.

4. **fix_error** — "The repair costed an arm and the leg."
   **Hint**: Use "cost" (past = "cost") and "an arm and a leg"
   **Answer**: The repair cost an arm and a leg.
   **Explanation**: Two corrections: "cost" is irregular (cost / cost / cost — no "costed"), and the idiom is "an arm and a leg" with article "a" twice. Both elements common at C1 — first as conjugation, second as fixed idiom form.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "She had been engaged for three months when she finally bit the bullet and admitted she was getting cold feet. Her sister, who never pulled her leg about anything serious, told her plainly: 'A wedding costs an arm and a leg. Calling it off costs less than staying in.'"
   **Question**: Which idiom in the passage means "to hesitate at the last moment"?
   **Answer**: getting cold feet
   **Accepts**: cold feet
   **Explanation**: "Cold feet" = hesitação de última hora, especialmente antes de um compromisso grande (casamento, decisão). Outros idioms no texto: "bit the bullet" (forçar-se), "pulled her leg" (provocar), "costs an arm and a leg" (caríssimo).

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"I'll just bite the bullet and tell her."** — face unpleasantness
2. **"He got cold feet about the move."** — last-minute hesitation
3. **"Are you pulling my leg?"** — checking truth of claim
4. **"It cost an arm and a leg, but worth it."** — very expensive
5. **"She's head over heels in love."** — completely smitten

## Unit A03 — Piece of cake, spill the beans

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Food idioms and difficulty/secret idioms (a piece of cake, spill the beans, take with a grain of salt, a tough nut to crack, that's the way the cookie crumbles) | **Tense**: IDIOM
> **Markers**: [denso] [idiom]
> **Real-life context**: You describe ease, difficulty, secrets, and acceptance using everyday food-based idioms.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "The interview was _____ — over in fifteen minutes."
   **Options**: a piece of cake / a piece of the cake / a slice of cake / piece cake
   **Answer**: a piece of cake
   **Explanation**: "A piece of cake" = very easy. Fixed article "a", not "the". Used to describe tasks that turned out simpler than feared. Origin: cake was associated with simple pleasures or as a prize for easy contests.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Don't _____ the beans before the announcement is official."
   **Options**: spill / pour / drop / leak
   **Answer**: spill
   **Explanation**: "Spill the beans" = reveal a secret, often prematurely or accidentally. Fixed verb "spill" + "the beans". Origin disputed (possibly ancient Greek voting with beans). Modern usage: any premature disclosure.

3. **fill_gap** — "Take what he says with a _____ of salt — he exaggerates."
   **Hint**: small unit of salt
   **Answer**: grain
   **Explanation**: "Take with a grain of salt" = treat with healthy scepticism. British variant: "pinch of salt". The image: a small dose of salt was once thought to neutralise poisons; metaphorically, a small dose of doubt neutralises misleading claims.

4. **fix_error** — "She's a hard nut to broke."
   **Hint**: "Crack" (not "broke"), and "tough" is more common than "hard"
   **Answer**: She's a tough nut to crack.
   **Accepts**: She's a hard nut to crack.
   **Explanation**: "Crack" is the fixed verb in this idiom — you "crack" a nut, not "break" it (although physically you do break shells, the idiom is fixed). "Tough" or "hard" both work, but the verb is non-negotiable.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "She had assumed the conversation would be a piece of cake — a quick update, no drama. Then he spilled the beans about the merger, told her to take half of what corporate said with a grain of salt, and warned her that the head of legal was a tough nut to crack. 'That's the way the cookie crumbles,' he said, smiling weakly."
   **Question**: How did he describe the head of legal?
   **Answer**: a tough nut to crack
   **Explanation**: Five food idioms packed into one passage: "a piece of cake" (easy), "spilled the beans" (revealed secret), "with a grain of salt" (sceptically), "a tough nut to crack" (hard person/problem), "the way the cookie crumbles" (acceptance of bad luck). Food idioms cluster naturally.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The exam was a piece of cake."** — task was easy
2. **"Don't spill the beans yet."** — keep the secret
3. **"Take that report with a grain of salt."** — be sceptical
4. **"He's a tough nut to crack."** — hard person to read
5. **"That's the way the cookie crumbles."** — accept the bad outcome

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M09, the student should:
- Use high-frequency conversational idioms in fitting contexts (hit the nail on the head, the elephant in the room, the tip of the iceberg, read between the lines, break the ice)
- Deploy body and emotion idioms (bite the bullet, get cold feet, pull someone's leg, cost an arm and a leg)
- Use food idioms (a piece of cake, spill the beans, take with a grain of salt, a tough nut to crack)
- Apply animal/strategy idioms (kill two birds with one stone, the ball is in your court, burn bridges, play it by ear, on the same page)
- Layer modern business metaphors (crystal clear, low-hanging fruit, a moving target, a double-edged sword, a blessing in disguise)
- Form similes correctly: "like + -ing form" or "like + noun phrase", never "like + to-infinitive"
- Avoid mixing metaphors within one clause

These figurative chunks set up M10 (Marcadores de discurso) — the connective tissue between sentences and paragraphs that completes the C1 fluency layer.
