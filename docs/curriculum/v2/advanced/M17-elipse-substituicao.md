# Module M17 — Ellipsis and substitution

> **Level**: Advanced (C2)
> **Block**: C2 Block
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Ellipsis (leaving things out), substitution with "do/so/one", "so do I" / "neither do I" patterns, "do so" vs "do it", verb-phrase ellipsis with auxiliaries
> **Module goal**: Student writes and speaks with the economy native speakers use — leaving out what's recoverable, substituting where repetition would be heavy.
> **Connects to**: M18 (Coesão em discurso extenso) — moves from clause-level economy to discourse-level cohesion.

## Module chunks introduced (~25)

- I want to, but I can't (ellipsis after "to")
- She doesn't think so / He hopes not (so/not substitution)
- I'd like a coffee — a strong one (one/ones substitution)
- He could have, but he didn't (auxiliary ellipsis)
- Do so / do it / do that (verb substitution)
- I love it, and so does he (so + aux inversion)
- I don't, and neither does she (neither + aux)
- Such things never end well (such substitution)
- The same goes for me
- I haven't read it. — Me neither.
- Yes, I am / No, I haven't (auxiliary as short answer)
- He was tired; she was too (too / either)

## Unit A01 — Ellipsis after "to"

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Verb-phrase ellipsis after "to" infinitive marker (I want to, but I can't — "go" omitted)
> **Markers**: [denso] [ellipsis]
> **Real-life context**: You leave out verb phrases that can be recovered from context — a hallmark of fluent English.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Are you coming to the meeting?" "I want to, but I _____."
   **Options**: can't / can't to / can't go / cannot
   **Answer**: can't
   **Explanation**: Ellipsis after modal: "I can't [come to the meeting]" — the verb phrase is omitted because it's recoverable from the question. "I can't to" is wrong (no "to" needed after modals). "I can't go" is grammatically correct but unnecessarily explicit; native speakers prefer the elliptical "I can't".

2. **multiple_choice** — "Will you call him?" "I'd like _____, but I don't have his number."
   **Options**: to / to call him / it / so
   **Answer**: to
   **Explanation**: Ellipsis after "to": "I'd like to [call him]" — the infinitive verb is omitted but "to" remains as the marker. Standard ellipsis pattern: "would like to / want to / mean to / hope to + ellipsis" — the "to" stays.

3. **fill_gap** — "Did you go to the party?" "I planned _____, but something came up."
   **Hint**: Infinitive marker retained
   **Answer**: to
   **Explanation**: "I planned to [go]" — verb omitted after "to". Same with "try to / hope to / mean to / intend to / expect to / be supposed to". The retained "to" is a signal that the verb phrase has been elided. C2 fluency uses this constantly.

4. **fix_error** — "I want go to the cinema." (Answering "Why aren't you coming?")
   **Hint**: Either include the full verb or use "to" + ellipsis
   **Answer**: I want to. (or: I want to go, but...)
   **Explanation**: "I want go" is ungrammatical — "want" requires "to + infinitive". For ellipsis: "I want to" (verb omitted, "to" kept). For full statement: "I want to go". Don't drop the "to" without dropping more.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "She had been invited to three events that evening. She would have gone to all of them if she could have. She would have liked to at least drop in on one. In the end, she didn't go to any. 'I meant to,' she said the next day. 'I just couldn't.'"
   **Question**: What word is omitted after "couldn't" in her final line?
   **Answer**: go (the verb is elided)
   **Explanation**: Four ellipses in five sentences: "if she could have [gone]", "She would have liked to [drop in / go]", "didn't go to any [of them]", "I meant to [go]", "I just couldn't [go]". Verb phrases recovered from context — fluent C2 economy.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"I want to, but I can't."** — ellipsis after modal
2. **"I'd like to."** — ellipsis after "to"
3. **"I meant to."** — ellipsis after "to"
4. **"I tried to."** — ellipsis after "to"
5. **"I hope to."** — ellipsis after "to"

## Unit A02 — So and not as substitutes

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: "So" and "not" replacing whole clauses (I think so / I don't think so / I'm afraid so / I hope not)
> **Markers**: [denso] [substitution]
> **Real-life context**: You replace entire affirmed or denied clauses with a single word — extremely common in conversation.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Is the train on time?" "I _____."
   **Options**: think so / think it / think yes / think to
   **Answer**: think so
   **Explanation**: "I think so" — "so" replaces the entire clause "that the train is on time". Common verbs: think, hope, believe, suppose, expect, guess. Negative: "I don't think so" (with "not" after the verb, not "I think not", although the latter exists in formal register).

2. **multiple_choice** — "Will it rain tomorrow?" "I hope _____."
   **Options**: not / not so / no / never
   **Answer**: not
   **Explanation**: "I hope not" — "not" replaces the negative version of the clause ("that it won't rain"). Same pattern: "I'm afraid not", "I guess not", "I suppose not". The negative substitute is "not" placed directly after the verb.

3. **fill_gap** — "Are you sure about the date?" "I _____, but let me double-check."
   **Hint**: "Believe so" or "think so"
   **Answer**: believe so (or "think so")
   **Explanation**: "I believe so" — substitution after believe. Same pattern with other thinking verbs: think, suppose, imagine, presume, gather. All take "so" or "not" as clause replacements in conversation.

4. **fix_error** — "Will she come? I think yes."
   **Hint**: Native English uses "I think so", not "I think yes"
   **Answer**: Will she come? I think so.
   **Explanation**: "I think yes" is direct translation from Portuguese ("Eu acho que sim") but isn't English. The English substitution is "so" after the thinking verb. "I think yes" sounds awkward; "I think so" is native.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The meeting was full of softened uncertainties. 'Will the deal close this quarter?' 'I think so.' 'Are we still on track for launch?' 'I hope so.' 'Is the CFO going to push back?' 'I'm afraid so.' 'And the board?' 'I expect not.' Four answers, each carrying just enough to keep the conversation moving."
   **Question**: How does the speaker respond to whether the board will push back?
   **Answer**: I expect not (he/she doesn't expect them to push back)
   **Explanation**: Four substitutions in five exchanges: "I think so", "I hope so", "I'm afraid so", "I expect not". Each replaces a whole clause. Conversation moves on without restating.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"I think so."** — affirm with substitute
2. **"I don't think so."** — deny with substitute
3. **"I hope not."** — negative wish
4. **"I'm afraid so."** — confirm bad news
5. **"I expect not."** — formal negative expectation

## Unit A03 — One, ones, that of

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: "One" / "ones" replacing nouns (a strong one, the red ones) and "that of / those of" (academic equivalent for X)
> **Markers**: [denso] [substitution]
> **Real-life context**: You avoid noun repetition by substituting "one/ones" in conversation and "that of/those of" in formal writing.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "I'd like a coffee — a _____."
   **Options**: strong one / strong / strong it / strong that
   **Answer**: strong one
   **Explanation**: "One" substitutes for a previously mentioned countable noun. "A coffee — a strong one" = "a strong coffee". The article "a" + adjective + "one" pattern. Avoids repeating "coffee".

2. **multiple_choice** — "Which dress do you prefer?" "The _____."
   **Options**: red one / red / red ones / red it
   **Answer**: red one
   **Explanation**: "The red one" — singular substitution with "one". For plural: "the red ones". The article ("the", "a") + adjective + one/ones is the standard substitution pattern. "The red" alone is incomplete in standard English (though heard in some dialects).

3. **fill_gap** — Academic: "The findings of this study contrast with _____ Smith (2020)."
   **Hint**: "those of"
   **Answer**: those of
   **Explanation**: "Those of + author/work" — academic substitution for "the findings of". "The findings of this study contrast with those of Smith (2020)" = "...contrast with the findings of Smith". Compresses by replacing "the findings of" with "those of". Formal register.

4. **fix_error** — "I want a coffee. I want a strong."
   **Hint**: Add "one" after the adjective
   **Answer**: I want a coffee. I want a strong one.
   **Explanation**: "A strong" alone is incomplete — "strong" needs a noun or a substitute. The noun "coffee" was just mentioned, so substitute it with "one". This is one of the most natural patterns in English; missing the "one" sounds like missing words.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The shop had every kind of bread. Brown ones, white ones, sourdough ones with crusts you could break a tooth on. I asked for the small one near the front, the one with seeds. The baker, who recognised me, said: 'Your usual one, then?' And I said yes."
   **Question**: What is repeated and substituted throughout the passage?
   **Answer**: bread / "one" / "ones" replace the noun "bread" repeatedly
   **Explanation**: Six "one/ones" substitutions in three sentences, each replacing "bread" or a specific bread. Native English avoids repeating the head noun by substituting "one/ones". Counted use is comfortable; novice writing would repeat the noun.

## Unit A04 — Do so / do it / do that

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Verb-phrase substitution with "do so" (formal), "do it" / "do that" (general), and "do likewise" / "do the same"
> **Markers**: [denso] [substitution]
> **Real-life context**: You replace whole verb phrases with do-substitutes — "I went to Madrid; she did the same" instead of "she went to Madrid too".

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Formal: "Please submit the application by Friday, and ensure your team _____ at the same time."
   **Options**: do so / does so / does it / does the same
   **Answer**: does so
   **Explanation**: "Do so" — formal verb-phrase substitute. Subject-verb agreement applies: "your team does so" (singular). "Do so" is most common in written/formal English. Less formal alternatives: "does it" or "does the same".

2. **multiple_choice** — General: "He apologised, and she _____ shortly after."
   **Options**: did the same / did so / does it / made the same
   **Answer**: did the same (or "did so")
   **Explanation**: "Did the same" — natural for the meaning "did what he did". "Did so" works too but is slightly more formal. The substitute replaces "apologised" so we don't repeat it. Both are valid C2 substitutions for verb phrases.

3. **fill_gap** — "I told her I would email Monday, but she suggested I _____ today instead."
   **Hint**: "Do it" or "do so"
   **Answer**: do so (or "do it")
   **Explanation**: "Do so" or "do it" — both replace "email" here. "Do so" leans formal; "do it" leans conversational. C2 speakers choose by register.

4. **fix_error** — "She walked into the room. He did so." (Stylistically — too repetitive of action)
   **Hint**: Native English often uses "He did the same" or "He did too"
   **Answer**: She walked into the room. He did the same. (or: She walked into the room. He did too.)
   **Explanation**: "He did so" is grammatical but stylistically heavy in narrative — feels formal-stilted. "Did the same" or "did too" or "followed" reads more naturally. Register-aware substitution: "do so" for formal, "do the same / do too" for everyday narrative.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "When the senior partner offered his resignation, the deputy did the same within the week. When pressed by the board, the chief financial officer initially refused; she would do so only after a private conversation with the chairman. By the end of the month, three of five executives had resigned, and the remaining two were widely expected to do likewise."
   **Question**: How many verb-phrase substitutions appear in the passage?
   **Answer**: three — "did the same" (resigned), "would do so" (refuse / give resignation), "do likewise" (resign)
   **Explanation**: Three substitutions for "resign": "did the same", "would do so", "do likewise". Each replaces the action verb to avoid repetition. Different forms suit different points in the paragraph.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"She apologised, and he did the same."** — match action
2. **"Please do so before Friday."** — formal instruction
3. **"I would do likewise in your position."** — formal echo
4. **"You can do it later."** — informal substitute
5. **"They were asked to do so immediately."** — passive substitute

## Unit A05 — So do I, neither do I

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Echoing agreement and disagreement with inverted auxiliaries (so do I, neither do I, so am I, neither was she)
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You echo someone's statement quickly and naturally — agreeing positively (so do I) or negatively (neither do I).

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "I love jazz." "_____."
   **Options**: So do I / Me do too / So am I / Same do I
   **Answer**: So do I
   **Explanation**: "So do I" — echo for positive statements. Pattern: "So + auxiliary + subject". Match the auxiliary to the original sentence: "I love" → "So do I". For "I am happy" → "So am I". For "I can swim" → "So can I". The auxiliary mirrors the original.

2. **multiple_choice** — "I haven't seen that film." "_____."
   **Options**: Neither have I / So have I / Either have I / Me neither
   **Answer**: Neither have I (or "Me neither" in casual register)
   **Explanation**: "Neither have I" — echo for negative statements. Pattern: "Neither + auxiliary + subject". Both work: "Neither have I" (formal) and "Me neither" (informal). Some speakers say "Nor have I" — synonymous with "Neither have I".

3. **fill_gap** — "She was tired." "_____."
   **Hint**: Echo with "so + was + subject"
   **Answer**: So was I (or "So was he" / "So was everyone")
   **Explanation**: Past simple of "be" → "So was + subject". Pattern: match the auxiliary form ("was") and invert with the subject. The whole verb phrase is replaced by the auxiliary echo.

4. **fix_error** — "I don't like coffee." "Me too."
   **Hint**: For negative agreement, use "Me neither" or "Neither do I"
   **Answer**: I don't like coffee. — Me neither. (or: Neither do I.)
   **Explanation**: "Me too" is for positive agreement ("I like coffee. — Me too"). For negative agreement, use "Me neither" or "Neither do I". Common L2 error — defaulting to "me too" for all agreement.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The dinner conversation had settled into rhythm. 'I haven't read his new book.' 'Neither have I.' 'I loved the last one, though.' 'So did I.' 'I won't be at the launch.' 'Nor will I — it's the night of my niece's recital.' By the end of the meal, they had agreed on more than they had disagreed on, even without arguing about anything."
   **Question**: How many echo-agreements appear?
   **Answer**: three — "Neither have I", "So did I", "Nor will I"
   **Explanation**: Three echoes in three exchanges: "Neither have I" (negative present perfect), "So did I" (positive past simple), "Nor will I" (negative future). Each mirrors the original auxiliary. Conversation flows by echo.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M17, the student should:
- Use ellipsis after "to" infinitive marker (I want to / I'd like to / I meant to)
- Substitute clauses with "so" and "not" (I think so / I hope not / I'm afraid so)
- Replace nouns with "one" / "ones" (a strong one / the red ones / the one with seeds)
- Use academic "those of" to compare findings (our findings vs those of Smith 2020)
- Replace verb phrases with "do so" (formal), "do it" / "do that" (general), or "do the same / do likewise"
- Echo positive statements with "So + auxiliary + subject" (So do I, So was I)
- Echo negative statements with "Neither / Nor + auxiliary + subject" (Neither have I, Nor will I)
- Distinguish "Me too" (positive) from "Me neither" (negative)
- Match auxiliary tense and aspect to the original sentence

M18 (Coesão em discurso extenso) takes economy from sentence/clause level to whole-discourse cohesion — keeping long texts hanging together.
