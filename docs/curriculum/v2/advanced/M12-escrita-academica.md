## Unit A01 — Nominalisation

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Converting verbs to nouns for academic compactness (the rise of, the decline in, the emergence of, an increase in)
> **Markers**: [denso] [academic]
> **Real-life context**: You write academic introductions and abstracts that pack ideas into noun phrases — characteristic of scientific and humanities prose.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Verbal: "Cities have grown rapidly over the last century." Nominalised: "_____ in the last century has reshaped global demographics."
   **Options**: The rapid growth of cities / Cities growing rapidly / That cities grew / Cities have grown
   **Answer**: The rapid growth of cities
   **Explanation**: Nominalisation converts a clause into a noun phrase: "Cities grew rapidly" → "the rapid growth of cities". This compresses information and shifts the focus from action to phenomenon. Common opener in academic introductions. Note: adjective "rapid" survives as modifier of the noun "growth".

2. **multiple_choice** — Verbal: "Sales have decreased recently." Nominalised: "_____ has affected our quarterly outlook."
   **Options**: The recent decrease in sales / Sales decreasing recently / That sales decreased / Sales have been decreasing
   **Answer**: The recent decrease in sales
   **Explanation**: "Decrease IN sales" — preposition "in" is the fixed link between the nominalised verb and the original subject. Other patterns: "rise IN", "decline IN", "increase IN", "shift IN/TOWARDS". The preposition is part of the chunk.

3. **fill_gap** — Verbal "Companies are adopting AI tools quickly." Nominalise: "_____ of AI tools by companies has accelerated."
   **Hint**: noun form of "adopt" + adjective
   **Answer**: The rapid adoption
   **Explanation**: "Adopt" → "adoption". Adjective "rapid" carries the adverbial sense ("quickly"). Original subject becomes prepositional ("BY companies"). Academic style favours such compressions for density and impersonal tone.

4. **fix_error** — "The increasing of climate awareness has changed policy."
   **Hint**: Use noun form, not gerund — "increase"
   **Answer**: The increase in climate awareness has changed policy.
   **Explanation**: "Increasing" is gerund/present participle. The nominalisation is "increase" (noun). Pattern: "the increase IN + topic". Don't use the verbal -ing form in nominalisation slots — it weakens the academic effect.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The opening paragraph of the paper read: 'The proliferation of digital platforms over the past two decades has driven a fundamental shift in how news is consumed. The decline in traditional print circulation has been matched, almost step for step, by the rise of algorithmic feeds. This paper examines the implications of that exchange.'"
   **Question**: According to the opening, what has matched the decline in print circulation?
   **Answer**: the rise of algorithmic feeds
   **Explanation**: Four nominalisations in three sentences: "the proliferation of platforms", "a shift in news consumption", "the decline in print circulation", "the rise of algorithmic feeds". Academic prose runs on nominalisation density.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The rise of digital platforms has reshaped media."** — opener nominalisation
2. **"The decline in attention spans is well documented."** — research-frame nominalisation
3. **"There has been a marked increase in remote work."** — quantified nominalisation
4. **"The emergence of new variants raised concern."** — scientific nominalisation
5. **"A shift towards plant-based diets is evident."** — direction nominalisation

## Unit A02 — Impersonal voice

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Removing first-person subjectivity (It is generally accepted that, It is widely held that, It is often argued that, this paper, the present study)
> **Markers**: [denso] [academic]
> **Real-life context**: You write in the impersonal scholarly voice — no "I think", no "I believe" — letting evidence and argument carry the claim.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Subjective: "I think climate change is human-driven." Academic: "_____ that climate change is human-driven."
   **Options**: It is widely accepted / I widely accept / Widely accepting / Wide acceptance
   **Answer**: It is widely accepted
   **Explanation**: Impersonal passive ("It is + past participle + that-clause") removes the speaker as agent. Common openers: "It is widely accepted / generally held / well established / often argued / increasingly recognised that...". Each calibrates how strong the consensus is.

2. **multiple_choice** — Subjective: "I argue that the model is flawed." Academic: "_____ that the model is flawed."
   **Options**: This paper argues / I am arguing / Argument is / Arguing is
   **Answer**: This paper argues
   **Explanation**: "This paper argues that..." or "The present study suggests..." — replacing "I" with the document or study as the agent. Standard academic convention to avoid first person. Other variants: "this analysis", "this article", "the present research".

3. **fill_gap** — Subjective: "I have found that students learn faster with feedback." Academic: "_____ that students learn faster with feedback."
   **Hint**: "The data" or "The results" + verb
   **Answer**: The data suggest (or "The results indicate" / "The findings show")
   **Explanation**: Academic prose lets the evidence speak. "The data suggest" — note plural verb (data is technically plural, though "data shows" is now common). "The findings indicate", "The results demonstrate" — all variations of the impersonal evidence-as-agent construction.

4. **fix_error** — Academic register: "I believe this hypothesis is correct."
   **Hint**: Replace "I believe" with impersonal alternative
   **Answer**: There is reason to believe this hypothesis is correct. (or: The evidence supports this hypothesis.)
   **Explanation**: "I believe" is too subjective for academic prose. Alternatives: "There is reason to believe", "The evidence supports", "It is plausible that", "It can be argued that". Each preserves the modesty of a claim while removing the speaker.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The introduction set the tone. 'This paper examines the relationship between sleep and creative output among knowledge workers. It is widely accepted that sleep affects cognition, yet relatively little is known about its effects on specifically creative tasks. The present study addresses this gap. The data suggest that even modest sleep restriction reduces divergent thinking by a measurable margin.'"
   **Question**: According to the introduction, what does the data suggest?
   **Answer**: even modest sleep restriction reduces divergent thinking (by a measurable margin)
   **Explanation**: Four impersonal frames in four sentences: "This paper examines...", "It is widely accepted that...", "The present study addresses...", "The data suggest that...". The author never appears as "I" — every claim has an institutional or evidential agent.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"It is widely accepted that exercise improves cognition."** — consensus framing
2. **"This paper argues for a revised approach."** — paper-as-agent
3. **"The data suggest a strong correlation."** — evidence-as-agent
4. **"There is reason to believe this is causal."** — hedged claim
5. **"The present study examines three variables."** — study-as-agent

## Unit A03 — Hedged claims

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Hedging strength of claim (this suggests, this implies, this indicates, the evidence points to, there is reason to believe, it could be argued)
> **Markers**: [denso] [academic]
> **Real-life context**: You signal precisely how confident a claim is — preserving intellectual honesty in academic writing.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Weak hedge: "_____ that the policy reduced unemployment."
   **Options**: This suggests / This proves / This shows definitively / We know
   **Answer**: This suggests
   **Explanation**: "This suggests that..." — moderate hedge. The evidence points in a direction but isn't conclusive. Stronger: "This demonstrates / This shows". Weaker: "This may suggest / It is conceivable that". C2 academic register calibrates hedge to actual evidence strength.

2. **multiple_choice** — Strong hedge: "_____ that the model has predictive power."
   **Options**: The evidence strongly supports / It is conceivable that / There is reason to believe / It might be the case that
   **Answer**: The evidence strongly supports
   **Explanation**: "The evidence strongly supports..." — high-confidence framing without claiming certainty. Academic prose almost never says "proves" — even strong findings are framed as "support", "indicate", "are consistent with". Caution is itself a virtue.

3. **fill_gap** — Hedged conclusion: "_____ that the relationship is causal, though further study is needed."
   **Hint**: "There is reason to believe"
   **Answer**: There is reason to believe
   **Explanation**: "There is reason to believe that..." — moderate-strong hedge often paired with "though further study is needed" or "but this remains to be confirmed". Pattern: hedge + qualifier. Marks intellectual humility while still advancing the claim.

4. **fix_error** — Academic register: "This proves that the drug works."
   **Hint**: Academic register avoids "proves" — use a hedged verb
   **Answer**: This suggests that the drug is effective. (or: The evidence supports the conclusion that the drug is effective.)
   **Explanation**: "Proves" implies certainty that scientific epistemology rarely warrants. Even strong findings use "supports", "suggests", "indicates", or "is consistent with". The hedge isn't false modesty — it's accurate representation of evidence.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The discussion section was characteristically hedged. 'The data suggest a meaningful association between sleep quality and creative output. This may indicate that improvements in one could yield improvements in the other. It is conceivable, though by no means established, that the relationship is bidirectional. Further longitudinal study would be needed to confirm the direction of causation.'"
   **Question**: How strong is the claim about bidirectional relationship?
   **Answer**: weak — described as conceivable but not established
   **Explanation**: Four graded hedges: "suggest" (moderate), "may indicate" (weaker), "It is conceivable, though by no means established" (very weak), "would be needed to confirm" (acknowledges open question). Hedging shows intellectual honesty about uncertainty.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"This suggests a strong correlation."** — moderate hedge
2. **"The evidence supports the hypothesis."** — strong supportive hedge
3. **"There is reason to believe this is causal."** — confident hedge
4. **"It is conceivable, though not established, that..."** — very weak hedge
5. **"The findings are consistent with prior research."** — alignment hedge

## Unit A04 — Citation patterns

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Citation framing (according to X (2020), as noted by, X argues that, X contends, building on prior work)
> **Markers**: [denso] [academic]
> **Real-life context**: You position your claim in the existing literature — citing, agreeing, contrasting, building.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Citing agreement: "_____ Smith (2020), early intervention is the strongest predictor of recovery."
   **Options**: According to / Following / In line / Like
   **Answer**: According to
   **Explanation**: "According to X (year), + claim" — standard citation frame for attributed assertions. Alternatives: "As X (year) argues" or "X (year) contends that". Each cites slightly differently — "according to" stays neutral; "X argues that" foregrounds X's position.

2. **multiple_choice** — Citing the agent: "_____ that genetic factors play a smaller role than previously assumed."
   **Options**: Wang (2019) argues / Wang argues 2019 / In 2019 Wang argued / By 2019 Wang argued
   **Answer**: Wang (2019) argues
   **Explanation**: Standard format: "Author (Year) verb that...". The year sits in parentheses immediately after the author's surname. Verbs vary by stance: "argues" (claim), "demonstrates" (with evidence), "suggests" (hedged), "contends" (often disputed by others).

3. **fill_gap** — Building on prior work: "_____ research on this topic, the present study extends the analysis to non-Western contexts."
   **Hint**: "Building on" or "Extending"
   **Answer**: Building on
   **Explanation**: "Building on + noun phrase" — academic frame for positioning your work as continuous with prior research. Alternatives: "Extending the work of", "Drawing on", "In line with". Each signals different relationship to the cited body.

4. **fix_error** — "Smith (2020) said that the policy failed."
   **Hint**: Replace "said" with academic citation verb
   **Answer**: Smith (2020) argues that the policy failed. (or: Smith (2020) contends that the policy failed.)
   **Explanation**: "Said" is too conversational for academic citation. Standard verbs: "argues", "contends", "claims", "demonstrates", "suggests", "posits", "asserts", "maintains", "concludes". Each carries different connotation about how strongly the claim is being defended.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The literature review built carefully. 'According to Chen (2018), the relationship between sleep and creativity has been understudied. Building on this observation, Park and Lee (2020) found a measurable effect on divergent thinking. Subsequent work by Müller (2022) contends that the effect is mediated by REM sleep specifically. The present study extends this line of inquiry to non-academic populations.'"
   **Question**: According to the passage, what does Müller (2022) contend?

## Unit A05 — Paragraph cohesion

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Topic sentences, paragraph progression, transition devices (this finding / this pattern / a related question / by contrast)
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You connect paragraphs so each follows naturally from the last — building cumulative academic argument.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Linking paragraph 2 to paragraph 1: "_____ pattern raises a related question — does the effect persist across cultures?"
   **Options**: This / That / Such / A
   **Answer**: This
   **Explanation**: "This + summary noun" is the classic academic paragraph-opener. "This pattern", "This finding", "This result", "This observation" — each gathers what was just said and pushes forward. Pronominal "this" + content noun = cohesion across paragraphs.

2. **multiple_choice** — Contrasting next paragraph: "_____, the second sample showed the opposite trend."
   **Options**: By contrast / On contrast / In contrast / At contrast
   **Answer**: By contrast
   **Explanation**: "By contrast, ..." opens a paragraph that presents opposing evidence or interpretation. Synonyms: "However, ...", "Conversely, ...", "On the other hand, ...". "By contrast" is academic-formal. Note: "in contrast TO + noun" is another pattern (different from "by contrast" as standalone).

3. **fill_gap** — Cumulative argument: "_____ findings together suggest a more general principle."
   **Hint**: "Taken together" or "Considered jointly"
   **Answer**: Taken together
   **Explanation**: "Taken together, these findings..." — academic frame for synthesising multiple results. Marks the cumulative move from individual evidence to general claim. Other patterns: "Considered jointly", "In aggregate", "Across the studies reviewed".

4. **fix_error** — Paragraph opener: "Also, the third experiment found similar results."
   **Hint**: "Also" is too casual to open an academic paragraph
   **Answer**: Furthermore, the third experiment yielded similar results. (or: A third experiment yielded similar results, reinforcing the pattern.)
   **Explanation**: "Also" is conversational. Academic register uses "Furthermore", "Moreover", "In addition", or restructures: "A third experiment...". Paragraph openers carry the connective load — too casual undercuts the register.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The article moved methodically. 'The first study found a small but consistent effect across all three age groups. This pattern raises a related question about gender differences, which the second study addressed. By contrast, the third study, conducted in a non-Western sample, produced a more complex picture. Taken together, these findings suggest that the underlying mechanism is robust, but its expression is culturally inflected.'"
   **Question**: What does the article conclude when the findings are taken together?
   **Answer**: the underlying mechanism is robust, but its expression is culturally inflected
   **Explanation**: Four cohesion devices in four sentences: "The first study found..." (anchoring), "This pattern raises a related question..." (carrying forward), "By contrast, the third study..." (signalling shift), "Taken together, these findings suggest..." (synthesising). Paragraph architecture at C2.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"This finding raises a related question."** — forward link
2. **"By contrast, the third sample diverged."** — contrast link
3. **"Taken together, these results suggest..."** — synthesis link
4. **"A related but distinct issue is..."** — adjacent link
5. **"Furthermore, the effect persists at follow-up."** — additive link

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M12, the student should:
- Nominalise verbal phrases for academic compactness (the rise of, the decline in, an increase in)
- Remove first person — let the paper/study/data be the agent
- Hedge claims to match evidence strength (suggests / indicates / supports / there is reason to believe / it is conceivable)
- Cite prior work in academic format (According to X (year), Wang (2019) argues, Building on...)
- Open paragraphs with cohesion devices (This pattern / By contrast / Furthermore / Taken together)
- Avoid contractions and casual transitions (also, so, but as paragraph openers)
- Choose academic verbs over conversational ones (postulate, contend, demonstrate instead of say, think, show casually)

M13 (Argumentação e persuasão) takes the academic register into argumentative writing — building a case, anticipating counterarguments, landing conclusions.
