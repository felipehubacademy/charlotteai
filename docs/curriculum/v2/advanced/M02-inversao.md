# Module M02 — Inversion

> **Level**: Advanced (C1)
> **Block**: C1 Block
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Inversion after negative/restrictive adverbials, time inversions, "only" inversions, emphatic inversions
> **Module goal**: Student produces and recognises inversion patterns that go beyond conditionals — for emphasis, formality, and literary effect.
> **Connects to**: M03 (Cleft sentences) — adjacent emphasis structures.

## Module chunks introduced (~22)

- Never have I + past participle
- Rarely do we / Seldom does he + base
- Hardly ever does she + base
- Hardly had I + past participle when...
- No sooner had I + past participle than...
- Scarcely had we...
- Not only did + subject + base, (but) also...
- Not until + clause + did + subject + base
- Only after / Only when / Only by + clause + did/do
- Little did + subject + know/realise/imagine
- Under no circumstances / On no account / In no way + auxiliary inversion

## Unit A01 — Never have I

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Inversion after negative frequency adverbials (Never, Rarely, Seldom, Hardly ever)
> **Markers**: [denso] [inversion]
> **Real-life context**: You front a negative adverb for emphasis, dramatising rarity or extreme experience.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Never _____ such a beautiful sunset."
   **Options**: have I seen / I have seen / did I saw / I saw
   **Answer**: have I seen
   **Explanation**: Fronting "Never" triggers subject-auxiliary inversion: auxiliary ("have") comes before the subject ("I"). The structure is "Never + auxiliary + subject + main verb". Compare to non-inverted "I have never seen..." — same meaning, less dramatic register.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Rarely _____ such commitment from a junior team."
   **Options**: do we see / we see / did we saw / we have see
   **Answer**: do we see
   **Explanation**: "Rarely" fronted requires "do/does/did + subject + base". Present tense general → "do we see". Without inversion: "We rarely see..." — flat. With inversion: emphasised observation.

3. **fill_gap** — "Seldom _____ a film leave me speechless the way that one did."
   **Hint**: "does" + base form
   **Answer**: does
   **Explanation**: "Seldom does a film leave..." — "does" auxiliary before subject "a film", then base form "leave". Singular subject takes "does".

4. **fix_error** — "Never I have been so insulted."
   **Hint**: Inversion requires auxiliary before subject
   **Answer**: Never have I been so insulted.
   **Explanation**: Fronted "Never" demands inversion. "Never I have been" mixes fronting with statement word order — neither natural sentence nor proper inversion. The corrected form moves the auxiliary "have" before "I".

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Reviewing the season, the coach was uncharacteristically blunt. 'Rarely have we played with so little discipline,' she said. 'Seldom does a team lose three games it should have won. We've earned exactly where we stand.'"
   **Question**: What does the coach criticize about the team?
   **Answer**: their lack of discipline (or "playing with little discipline" or "losing winnable games")
   **Explanation**: Two fronted inversions ("Rarely have we played" and "Seldom does a team lose") emphasise the rarity — and weight — of the criticism. The coach is using formal-public register.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Never have I seen such a sunset."** — emphatic personal record
2. **"Rarely do we hear such honesty."** — emphatic observation
3. **"Seldom does she ask for help."** — habitual rarity
4. **"Hardly ever does it rain in July here."** — climate generalization
5. **"Never have I felt so understood."** — emotional emphasis

## Unit A02 — Hardly had I, No sooner had I

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Inversion after "Hardly", "Scarcely", "No sooner" for near-simultaneous past events
> **Markers**: [denso] [inversion]
> **Real-life context**: You narrate two past events as overlapping — one barely begun when the next intervenes.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Hardly _____ down when the phone rang."
   **Options**: had I sat / I had sat / did I sat / I sat
   **Answer**: had I sat
   **Explanation**: "Hardly + had + subject + past participle + when + past simple". Two near-simultaneous past events: I'd just sat down, and immediately the phone rang. "When" links the second clause.

2. **multiple_choice** — "No sooner _____ the project _____ the deadline changed."
   **Options**: had we finished / than / did we finish / when / had we finished / when / did we finish / than
   **Answer**: had we finished / than
   **Explanation**: "No sooner + had + subject + past participle + than + past simple". Note "THAN" not "when" — that's the trap. "No sooner had we finished than the deadline changed."

3. **fill_gap** — "Scarcely _____ I _____ the door when the rain started."
   **Hint**: had / past participle of "shut"
   **Answer**: had / shut
   **Explanation**: "Scarcely had I shut the door when..." — same pattern as "Hardly". Past perfect + when + past simple. Slight literary register.

4. **fix_error** — "Hardly I had sat down when the phone rang."
   **Hint**: Inversion required after "Hardly"
   **Answer**: Hardly had I sat down when the phone rang.
   **Explanation**: "Hardly" fronted demands subject-auxiliary inversion: "had I" not "I had". Without inversion the structure reads as a hesitant assertion, not the intended near-simultaneity.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The novelist wrote of her childhood: 'Hardly had we settled into the new town when my father announced another move. No sooner had I made a friend than I was packing again. By twelve I had lived in seven houses and counted only my books as steady company.'"
   **Question**: How frequently did the family move?
   **Answer**: very often (or "every time she made a friend" or "many times — seven houses by age 12")
   **Explanation**: Two stacked inversions ("Hardly had we settled... No sooner had I made a friend") dramatise the rapid succession of moves. The structures are narrative engines for back-to-back events.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Hardly had I sat down when the phone rang."** — interrupted rest
2. **"No sooner had we left than it started raining."** — bad timing
3. **"Scarcely had he spoken when she walked out."** — quick reaction
4. **"Hardly had I closed my eyes when the alarm went off."** — false dawn
5. **"No sooner had I finished one project than another landed."** — work cascade

## Unit A03 — Not only, Not until

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Inversion after "Not only" (additive emphasis) and "Not until" (delayed realization)
> **Markers**: [denso] [inversion]
> **Real-life context**: You stack achievements/qualities with "not only" or dramatise late realisation with "not until".

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Not only _____ the marathon, but she also raised £10,000 for charity."
   **Options**: did she finish / she finished / finished she / had she finish
   **Answer**: did she finish
   **Explanation**: "Not only" fronted triggers inversion: "did + subject + base". The "but also" clause introduces a second, layered fact. The structure dramatises the layering.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Not until I saw the report _____ the scale of the problem."
   **Options**: did I understand / I understood / understood I / I have understood
   **Answer**: did I understand
   **Explanation**: "Not until + clause + did + subject + base". The fronted "not until" inverts the main clause that follows. Past simple realisation triggered by past simple cue.

3. **fill_gap** — "Not only _____ he _____ on time, but he also brought everyone coffee."
   **Hint**: "did" (past) / "arrive"
   **Answer**: did / arrive
   **Explanation**: "Not only did he arrive" — past, so "did + subject + base". Followed by "but he also + verb" — no inversion in the second clause.

4. **fix_error** — "Not only she sings, but she also writes her own songs."
   **Hint**: "Not only" demands inversion
   **Answer**: Not only does she sing, but she also writes her own songs.
   **Explanation**: Fronted "Not only" requires "does + subject + base" (present singular). Without inversion the sentence reads as fragmented assertion, not balanced emphasis.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The award citation read: 'Not only has Dr. Lima reshaped the field through her research, but she has also mentored a generation of scientists. Not until we examined her full body of work did we appreciate how interconnected her contributions truly are.'"
   **Question**: What two achievements does the citation emphasise?
   **Answer**: research that reshaped the field + mentoring a generation (or similar)
   **Explanation**: "Not only... but also" layers two achievements; "Not until... did we appreciate" dramatises late recognition. Both inversions register formality and emphasis.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Not only did she win, but she also broke the record."** — layered triumph
2. **"Not only is he kind, but he's also brilliant."** — layered praise
3. **"Not until I read it twice did I get the joke."** — delayed comprehension
4. **"Not until then did I realise how tired I was."** — delayed awareness
5. **"Not only have we cut costs, but we've also raised quality."** — corporate-style layering

## Unit A04 — Only after, Little did I know

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Inversion after "Only after/when/by" (delayed cause) and "Little did + subject + know/realise" (dramatic irony)
> **Markers**: [denso] [inversion]
> **Real-life context**: You write or speak with literary flair — marking when realisation arrived (or didn't).

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Only after the meeting _____ what she had meant."
   **Options**: did I understand / I understood / understood I / I have understood
   **Answer**: did I understand
   **Explanation**: "Only after + clause + did + subject + base". The cue clause ("after the meeting") triggers inversion in the main clause. Dramatises the delay.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Only by working together _____ we _____ this on time."
   **Options**: will / finish / can / finish / would / finished / are / finishing
   **Answer**: will / finish
   **Explanation**: "Only by + -ing + will + subject + base". The "only by" prepositional phrase fronts and inverts. Indicates the sole pathway to a future outcome.

3. **fill_gap** — "Little _____ he _____ that the offer was about to be withdrawn."
   **Hint**: "did" / past simple base "know"
   **Answer**: did / know
   **Explanation**: "Little did he know" — classic dramatic irony opener. "Little + did/does + subject + base" expresses unawareness of something soon revealed. "Little did I realise/imagine" follows the same pattern.

4. **fix_error** — "Only when I left home, I realised how much I missed it."
   **Hint**: "Only when + clause" requires inversion in main
   **Answer**: Only when I left home did I realise how much I missed it.
   **Explanation**: Both halves were in flat order. "Only when" fronted means the main clause needs "did I realise", not "I realised". The inversion marks the realisation as cued by the leaving.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Looking back, the entrepreneur reflected: 'Only by losing my first company did I learn what entrepreneurship actually requires. Little did I know at twenty-five that failure would teach me what success never could.'"
   **Question**: What lesson does the entrepreneur say only failure could teach?
   **Answer**: what entrepreneurship actually requires (or "what success couldn't teach")
   **Explanation**: "Only by losing... did I learn" — inversion after restrictive phrase. "Little did I know... that failure would teach me" — inversion of dramatic irony. Both common in retrospective narrative.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Only after the meeting did I get her point."** — delayed understanding
2. **"Only by trying did I learn what I could do."** — pathway through action
3. **"Little did I know it would change my life."** — dramatic foreshadowing
4. **"Only when she spoke did I notice the accent."** — sensory delay
5. **"Little did he realise the door was already locked."** — unawareness reveal

## Unit A05 — Under no circumstances

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Inversion after restrictive negative phrases (Under no circumstances, On no account, In no way, At no time)
> **Markers**: [denso] [inversion]
> **Real-life context**: You write formal policy, contracts, or assert firm boundaries with elevated emphasis.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Under no circumstances _____ leave the building during the drill."
   **Options**: should you / you should / you must / shall you
   **Answer**: should you
   **Explanation**: "Under no circumstances + modal + subject + base". The fronted negative phrase forces inversion. Common in safety notices and formal instructions.

2. **multiple_choice** — "On no account _____ this door."
   **Options**: must you open / you must open / open you / are you opening
   **Answer**: must you open
   **Explanation**: "On no account + must + subject + base". Strong prohibition. Note the structure persists: fronted negative phrase, then auxiliary, then subject.

3. **fill_gap** — "At no time _____ the company _____ that policy."
   **Hint**: "did" or "has" / "endorse/endorsed"
   **Answer**: did / endorse
   **Explanation**: "At no time did the company endorse..." — past inversion. Also acceptable: "At no time has the company endorsed..." for present perfect denial. Both register formal disavowal.

4. **fix_error** — "In no way she meant to offend you."
   **Hint**: "In no way" fronted requires inversion
   **Answer**: In no way did she mean to offend you.
   **Explanation**: "In no way" is a fronted negative phrase. Inversion required: "did + subject + base". Without inversion the sentence reads as a half-formed thought.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The corporate code of conduct is unambiguous: 'Under no circumstances shall an employee share confidential client information with third parties. On no account may personal devices be used to store such data. At no time has this organisation tolerated breaches of trust.'"
   **Question**: What does the code prohibit?
   **Answer**: sharing confidential client information with third parties (or "storing it on personal devices")
   **Explanation**: Three stacked formal inversions ("Under no circumstances shall... On no account may... At no time has...") amplify the policy's seriousness. Corporate-formal register.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Under no circumstances should you open that file."** — strict prohibition
2. **"On no account must we miss the deadline."** — emphatic warning
3. **"At no time did I authorise that purchase."** — formal denial
4. **"In no way is this acceptable."** — outraged formal
5. **"Under no circumstances will we lower our standards."** — corporate commitment

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M02, the student should:
- Trigger inversion after Never, Rarely, Seldom, Hardly ever (negative frequency)
- Pair "Hardly... when" / "Scarcely... when" / "No sooner... than" with past perfect + past simple
- Front "Not only... but also" and "Not until... did" for layered or delayed emphasis
- Use "Only after / Only when / Only by + clause" with inversion in the main clause
- Open dramatic-irony narrative with "Little did I know/realise/imagine"
- Frame formal prohibitions with "Under no circumstances / On no account / At no time / In no way"
- Distinguish "than" vs "when" in time inversions (No sooner... than; Hardly... when)

These structures feed directly into M03 (Cleft sentences) — another emphasis system that complements inversion.
