# Module M21 — Speak like a native

> **Level**: Advanced (C2)
> **Block**: C2 Block
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Discourse fillers (you know, I mean, like), hedge markers (kind of, sort of, basically), tag questions (isn't it? right?), backchannels (mm-hm, right, gotcha), pragmatic openers (honestly, to be fair, no offence)
> **Module goal**: Student speaks with the texture of fluent native speech — uses fillers and hedges deliberately, signals listening, opens delicate topics with the right pragmatic markers.

## Unit A01 — Discourse fillers

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Filler words used deliberately (you know, I mean, like, sort of) — for thinking time, hedging, register softening | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: [denso] [pragmatic]
> **Real-life context**: You use fillers strategically — never overusing them, but sprinkling them in to sound natural rather than rehearsed.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Which sentence sounds most natural in casual speech?
   **Options**: It was, you know, a difficult situation. / It was a difficult situation. / It was, sort of, you know, I mean, a difficult situation. / It was difficult.
   **Answer**: It was, you know, a difficult situation.
   **Explanation**: "You know" — one well-placed filler signals casual register and gives the speaker thinking time. Two are still natural; three or more sound nervous or under-rehearsed. C2 fluency means knowing when one filler helps and when more start to hurt.

2. **multiple_choice** — Most natural use of "I mean":
   **Options**: I mean, the meeting was a disaster. / I mean, I mean, I mean, the meeting was bad. / I mean, you know, sort of, the meeting was bad. / The meeting, I mean.
   **Answer**: I mean, the meeting was a disaster.
   **Explanation**: "I mean" — opens a clarification or emphatic restatement. Functions as a soft topic-anchor in casual speech. Common at the start of a sentence or after a pause. Native speakers use it constantly; learners often miss it entirely or overuse it.

3. **fill_gap** — Hedge in casual speech: "She's _____ the best manager I've ever had."
   **Hint**: "Kind of" or "sort of"
   **Answer**: kind of
   **Accepts**: sort of
   **Explanation**: "Kind of" / "sort of" — hedges. Soften the absoluteness of "the best". "Kind of the best" = approximately the best, hesitant to claim full superlative. In writing this hedge is light; in speech it's heavy and frequent.

4. **fix_error** — Casual speech, but over-filled: "Like, you know, I mean, like, the project was, sort of, really hard, you know?"
   **Hint**: Three or more fillers in one sentence is too many
   **Answer**: The project was really hard, you know?
   **Explanation**: Over-filling sounds nervous or under-prepared. C2 fluency = strategic filler use. One filler per sentence is natural; two is borderline; three or more weakens. Remove some and keep one well-placed.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Native speech uses fillers as semantic glue. 'It was, you know, a tough call' = the speaker is signalling casual register, sharing a moment of judgement-formation. 'I mean, who would have predicted that?' = the speaker is shifting register or correcting course. 'She's kind of new at this' = the speaker is softening an evaluation. Each filler does specific work."
   **Question**: According to the passage, what specific work does 'kind of' do in 'She's kind of new at this'?
   **Answer**: softening an evaluation
   **Explanation**: Three fillers, three functions: "you know" = casual signal, "I mean" = register shift/correction, "kind of" = softening. Native fluency uses them deliberately. Avoiding them entirely sounds stiff; using them randomly sounds incoherent.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

3. **"She's kind of new at this."** — softening hedge

## Unit A02 — Tag questions

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Tag questions (isn't it? aren't they? hasn't she? right? yeah?) — agreement-seeking, confirmation, rhetorical | **Tense**: TAG QUESTION
> **Markers**: [denso] [pragmatic]
> **Real-life context**: You add tags to statements to seek agreement, soften assertion, or invite conversation.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Add the correct tag: "It's a beautiful day, _____"
   **Options**: isn't it? / is it? / isn't is? / it isn't?
   **Answer**: isn't it?
   **Explanation**: Tag question rule: positive statement + negative tag. "It's / It is beautiful" (positive, "is") → "isn't / is not it?" (negative). Tags reverse polarity. Common pattern: "She's coming, isn't / is not she?" / "They've left, haven't / have not they?" / "You like coffee, don't / do not you?". Agreement-seeking move.

2. **multiple_choice** — Add the correct tag: "She doesn't speak French, _____"
   **Options**: does she? / doesn't she? / is she? / she does?
   **Answer**: does she?
   **Explanation**: Negative statement → positive tag. "She doesn't / does not speak" (negative, "doesn't / does not") → "does she?" (positive). Reverse polarity. Common error: matching the polarity. The point of the tag is to flip and check.

3. **fill_gap** — Casual tag for "It's good weather today, _____" (very informal British)
   **Hint**: Universal informal tag
   **Answer**: innit
   **Accepts**: isn't it" formal
   **Explanation**: "Innit" — universal informal tag in British English, especially Cockney/London. Stands in for any positive→negative tag ("isn't it / aren't they / haven't they / didn't he"). Use cautiously — works in casual peer-to-peer speech but reads as too informal in professional contexts.

4. **fix_error** — Tag mismatched: "You've been to Paris, haven't you?"
   **Hint**: Actually, that's correct. Try: "You haven't been to Paris, have you?"
   **Answer**: You haven't been to Paris, have you? (negative → positive tag)
   **Explanation**: Common L2 error is matching polarity. Native English always reverses: pos → neg tag, neg → pos tag. "You've / You have been" (positive perfect) + "haven't / have not you?" (negative). "You haven't / have not been" (negative) + "have you?" (positive). Polarity always inverts.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Tags do different work depending on intonation. 'It's a lovely day, isn't it?' with rising tag = genuinely asking, expecting agreement. With falling tag = rhetorical, not really seeking confirmation. Tags can also soften assertion: 'You'd rather not come, would you?' is gentler than 'You don't want to come.' English speech relies on these constantly."
   **Question**: According to the passage, what makes the difference between a genuine question tag and a rhetorical one?
   **Answer**: intonation — rising for genuine, falling for rhetorical
   **Explanation**: Tag question carries function via intonation: rising = real question, expecting agreement; falling = rhetorical, not really expecting answer. C2 mastery includes producing both. Also tags soften statements that would otherwise feel blunt or accusatory.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

3. **"You've been there, haven't you?"** — present perfect tag
4. **"That was a mistake, right?"** — universal "right" tag
5. **"Lovely view, innit?"** — informal British tag

## Unit A03 — Backchannels

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Listening signals (mm-hm, right, yeah, okay, gotcha, oh, really, I see, no way) — confirming you're tracking the speaker | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: [denso] [pragmatic]
> **Real-life context**: You signal active listening through small interjections — without interrupting the speaker. Critical for casual fluency.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Most natural backchannel during someone telling a story:
   **Options**: Mm-hm. Right. Yeah. / Yes, I am listening. / I understand what you're saying. / Continue, please.
   **Answer**: Mm-hm. Right. Yeah.
   **Explanation**: Native speakers signal listening with short interjections — not full sentences. "Mm-hm", "right", "yeah", "okay", "oh", "gotcha" — each is a small confirmation that the listener is tracking. Long acknowledgements ("I understand what you're / you are saying") interrupt and feel formal.

2. **multiple_choice** — Backchannel signalling surprise:
   **Options**: Really? / Yes, indeed. / I see your point. / Acknowledged.
   **Answer**: Really?
   **Explanation**: "Really?" — surprise backchannel. Synonyms: "No way", "Oh", "Seriously?". Each invites the speaker to elaborate. Native conversation depends on these small expressions of receptivity. Without them, speakers feel their story isn't landing.

3. **fill_gap** — Backchannel meaning "I'm tracking, keep going": "_____"
   **Hint**: Single syllable, neutral confirmation
   **Answer**: Right
   **Accepts**: Mm-hm / Yeah
   **Explanation**: "Right" — neutral confirmation backchannel. Often timed during pauses or at the end of a sentence. Allows the speaker to continue without seeking more elaborate confirmation. Stress-pattern: short, rising-falling pitch, almost throwaway.

4. **fix_error** — Over-elaborate backchannel: "I am listening to your account with great interest."
   **Hint**: Use a short interjection instead
   **Answer**: Mm-hm.
   **Accepts**: Right. / Yeah. / Oh.
   **Explanation**: Formal acknowledgement reads as ironic or alien in casual speech. Native fluency uses short backchannels. "I am listening with interest" in casual chat = either sarcasm or non-native flag. C2 fluency replaces it with one-syllable confirmation.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Native conversation has its own rhythm. The speaker tells the story. The listener punctuates with quiet sounds — 'mm-hm... right... yeah... oh, really?... no way.' These tiny words do enormous work. They tell the speaker the listener is present, paying attention, and emotionally tracking. Without them, the speaker feels alone on the line."
   **Question**: According to the passage, what work do backchannels do?
   **Answer**: tell the speaker the listener is present, paying attention, and emotionally tracking
   **Explanation**: Three functions: presence, attention, emotional tracking. All carried by single-word interjections. C2 conversational mastery includes producing these naturally — and noticing when other speakers go silent (a signal to wrap up or shift).

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Mm-hm."** — neutral confirmation
2. **"Right."** — short acknowledgement
3. **"Really?"** — surprise invitation
4. **"No way."** — strong surprise
5. **"Gotcha."** — informal understanding signal

## Unit A04 — Pragmatic openers

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Honest / honestly / frankly / to be fair / no offence / look — pragmatic markers that open sensitive turns | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: [denso] [pragmatic]
> **Real-life context**: You preface what you're about to say with a marker that signals tone — honest, fair, gentle, or about to disagree.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Opener signalling about-to-share-an-honest-opinion: "_____, the proposal isn't strong enough."
   **Options**: Honestly / Yes / Look here / Apologies
   **Answer**: Honestly
   **Explanation**: "Honestly, ..." — opens a candid statement. Slightly informal. Synonyms: "Frankly, ...", "To be honest, ...", "I'll / I will be honest with you, ...". Marks that the speaker is dropping diplomatic cover. Common in feedback or honest assessments.

2. **multiple_choice** — Opener softening disagreement before a counterpoint: "_____, the data does support a different reading."
   **Options**: To be fair / Of course / Listen here / In all honesty
   **Answer**: To be fair
   **Explanation**: "To be fair, ..." — opens a balancing counterpoint. Signals "I want to give credit to the other side". Synonyms: "In fairness, ...". Used to defuse before disagreeing or to add nuance to an unbalanced statement.

3. **fill_gap** — Casual opener for sensitive topic: "_____, I think we should reconsider."
   **Hint**: Attention-getter with deliberate pause
   **Answer**: Look
   **Explanation**: "Look, ..." — informal attention-getter, often preceding a sensitive or direct statement. Casual register. Synonyms: "Listen, ...". Signals "I'm / I am about to be direct" — used in interpersonal conversation, feedback, or persuasion.

4. **fix_error** — Awkward sensitivity-opener: "With no offence, but you're wrong."
   **Hint**: "No offence" is the fixed phrase
   **Answer**: No offence, but you're wrong.
   **Accepts**: With all due respect, but you're wrong.
   **Explanation**: "No offence" — set phrase preceding a potentially insulting statement. "With no offence" is non-standard. The phrase preempts any negative reaction: "I'm / I am not trying to insult you, but...". Often softens what follows.

   **Question**: According to the passage, what does 'Look, we need to talk' signal?
   **Answer**: seriousness
   **Explanation**: Four openers, four tonal preparations: "Honestly" (candour), "To be fair" (balance), "No offence" (preemptive diplomacy), "Look" (seriousness). Each tells the listener how to receive what comes next. C2 fluency uses these consciously.

## Unit A05 — The thing is, here's the thing

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Problem-naming openers (the thing is / here's the thing / look / I'll tell you what / I don't know about you but) — native conversational moves | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You introduce a complication, observation, or proposal with a native conversational opener.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Opener for naming a complication: "_____ we don't actually have the budget for it this quarter."
   **Options**: The thing is, / The thing of, / In the thing, / Thing is to,
   **Answer**: The thing is,
   **Explanation**: "The thing is, ..." — opens a complication or important point. Casual, conversational. Variant: "Here's the thing, ...". Used to slow down a conversation and name the actual obstacle. Common in feedback, negotiation, and explanations.

2. **multiple_choice** — Native conversational proposal opener:
   **Options**: I'll tell you what — let's just call her and ask. / I will tell you which — let's call her. / Tell I you what — call her. / I'll tell you so — call.
   **Answer**: I'll tell you what — let's just call her and ask.
   **Explanation**: "I'll / I will tell you what, ..." or "Tell you what, ..." — opens a casual proposal. Comes with the rhythm of someone arriving at an idea on the spot. Common in spoken English; rare in writing.

3. **fill_gap** — Opener inviting shared opinion: "_____ but I think we should rethink the whole approach."
   **Hint**: "I don't know about you, but..."
   **Answer**: I don't know about you,
   **Explanation**: "I don't / do not know about you, but ..." — opens a personal opinion in a way that invites the listener's response. Hedges the assertion as personal. Common in casual feedback and informal opinion-sharing.

4. **fix_error** — Awkward: "The matter is that we don't have time."
   **Hint**: Use "The thing is" instead
   **Answer**: The thing is, we don't have time.
   **Explanation**: "The matter is" sounds formal-translated. Native English casual conversation uses "The thing is" or "Here's the thing". These openers feel native; "the matter is" reads as translation from another language.

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M21, the student should:
- Sprinkle discourse fillers (you know, I mean, like) strategically — one per sentence, not three
- Hedge with kind of / sort of / basically / essentially
- Form correct tag questions with reversed polarity (pos → neg tag, neg → pos tag)
- Backchannel during others' speech with short interjections (mm-hm, right, yeah, gotcha, really?, no way) — never full sentences
- Open delicate turns with pragmatic markers (honestly, to be fair, no offence, look, frankly)
- Use native problem-naming openers (the thing is, here's the thing, I'll tell you what, I don't know about you but)
- Calibrate register: more fillers and contractions in casual, fewer in formal
- Avoid sounding either rehearsed (no fillers, all formal) or under-prepared (too many fillers)

M22 (Síntese e mastery) closes the entire curriculum — integrating every C1/C2 skill into a final synthesis.
