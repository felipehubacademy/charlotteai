> **Level**: Advanced (C1)
> **Block**: C1 Block
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Advanced discourse markers — nuance (in fact, as a matter of fact), concession (that said, mind you), reformulation (in other words, put differently), topic shifts (incidentally, by the way), emphatic chains (what's more, not least, above all)

## Unit A01 — In fact, as a matter of fact

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Nuancing markers (in fact, as a matter of fact, actually, indeed) — strengthening or correcting an earlier claim
> **Markers**: [denso] [discourse]
> **Real-life context**: You sharpen a previous statement, add a surprising detail, or politely correct an interlocutor.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "I thought the meeting was Wednesday. _____, it's Thursday."
   **Options**: As a matter of fact / On a matter of fact / By a matter of fact / In matter of fact
   **Answer**: As a matter of fact
   **Explanation**: "As a matter of fact" introduces a correction or surprising clarification, often politely. Fixed prepositional phrase. Synonyms: "in fact", "actually". Used to update someone's understanding without sounding confrontational. The marker softens the correction.

2. **multiple_choice** — "She didn't just like the proposal — _____, she championed it to the board."
   **Options**: in fact / on fact / for fact / by fact
   **Answer**: in fact
   **Explanation**: "In fact" strengthens or upgrades a previous claim. Often used to escalate: not just X, in fact Y (something stronger). Punctuation: comma after when fronted. "In fact" can intensify, while "as a matter of fact" tends to correct or clarify.

3. **fill_gap** — "We weren't expecting an offer that high. _____, we were ready to negotiate for less."
   **Hint**: Past clarification marker
   **Answer**: As a matter of fact
   **Explanation**: "As a matter of fact" introduces a behind-the-scenes truth that softens or surprises. Used when adding context that adjusts the interlocutor's mental model.

4. **fix_error** — "Actually, I don't think that's correct, in fact."
   **Hint**: Don't stack both nuancing markers in the same clause
   **Answer**: Actually, I don't think that's correct.
   **Accepts**: In fact, I don't think that's correct.
   **Explanation**: "Actually" and "in fact" are near-synonyms. Stacking them creates redundancy. Pick one. C1 prose values economy — repetition of equivalent markers reads as nervous overcorrection.

5. **multiple_choice** — "The press release: 'The board respects the founder's decision. In fact, we asked her to take this step several weeks ago. As a matter of fact, the timing of her announcement was agreed jointly. Actually, the only surprise here is that the press caught up so quickly.' — What does this press release ULTIMATELY imply about the founder's decision?"
   **Options**: The board had asked her to step down / She acted entirely on her own / The board opposed her decision / The decision was sudden and unplanned
   **Answer**: The board had asked her to step down
   **Explanation**: Os três marcadores ("In fact", "As a matter of fact", "Actually") reformulam progressivamente a narrativa: do "respect" inicial pra revelar que a saída foi pedida pelo board. Esse é o jogo retórico do C1 — marcadores que aparentam neutralidade carregam afirmação forte.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"In fact, she championed it to the board."** — escalation
2. **"As a matter of fact, it's Thursday."** — gentle correction
3. **"Actually, I'd prefer the earlier slot."** — soft alternative
4. **"Indeed, that's exactly what we found."** — formal confirmation
5. **"In fact, the opposite turned out to be true."** — reversal

## Unit A02 — That said, mind you

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Concession markers (that said, having said that, mind you, then again, granted, admittedly) — acknowledging the other side
> **Markers**: [denso] [discourse]
> **Real-life context**: You build balanced argument by signalling that you've considered counterpoints — preventing your view from sounding one-sided.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Remote work has real upsides. _____, it can be isolating."
   **Options**: That said / That so said / Said that / In that said
   **Answer**: That said
   **Explanation**: "That said" introduces a concession after a positive claim. Fixed phrase, comma after. Synonymous with "having said that". Marks that the speaker is acknowledging the counterpoint without abandoning the main position. Essential C1 balance.

2. **multiple_choice** — "She's brilliant. _____, she can be hard to work with."
   **Options**: Mind you / Mind that / Mind for / Mind to
   **Answer**: Mind you
   **Explanation**: "Mind you" — informal-conversational concession, common in British English. Drops in a counterpoint that adjusts your assessment. Used when sharing nuanced opinions in casual register. Punctuation: comma after.

3. **fill_gap** — "_____, I might have done the same thing in his position."
   **Hint**: "Granted" or "Admittedly" or "Then again"
   **Answer**: Then again
   **Explanation**: "Then again" introduces a reconsideration — second-guessing your own assertion. Punctuation: comma after. Slightly more reflective than "that said"; suggests the speaker is genuinely reconsidering, not just balancing.

4. **fix_error** — "She's talented. However, that said, she's also lazy."
   **Hint**: "However" and "that said" do the same work — don't stack
   **Answer**: She's talented. That said, she's also lazy.
   **Accepts**: She's talented. However, she's also lazy.
   **Explanation**: "However" and "that said" both mark concession. Stacking is redundant. C1 register prefers one well-placed marker. Choose by tone: "however" reads more formal-written, "that said" more conversational.

5. **multiple_choice** — "She made a strong case for the move. Granted, the new market is competitive. Mind you, it's also growing twice as fast as our current one. Admittedly, our team would need to relocate or hire from scratch. That said, the cost of inaction is starting to look higher than the cost of action. — What does the speaker ultimately favour?"
   **Options**: Making the move / Staying put / Hiring more local staff / Delaying the decision
   **Answer**: Making the move
   **Explanation**: Os quatro marcadores de concessão ("Granted", "Mind you", "Admittedly", "That said") cedem pontos individualmente mas culminam em "the cost of inaction is higher than the cost of action" — conclusão a favor da mudança. Arquitetura argumentativa C1.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"That said, the risks are real."** — concession after positive
2. **"Mind you, she's been right before."** — informal counterpoint
3. **"Then again, the timing could be perfect."** — reconsidering
4. **"Granted, the budget is tight."** — formal yield
5. **"Admittedly, I didn't see that coming."** — yielding to a fact

## Unit A03 — In other words, put differently

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Reformulation markers (in other words, that is to say, put differently, to put it another way, by which I mean, or rather)
> **Markers**: [denso] [discourse]
> **Real-life context**: You restate complex ideas more simply, recast a phrase, or correct yourself in real time.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "The market is mean-reverting. _____, prices tend to swing back to their average."
   **Options**: In other words / On other words / By other words / With other words
   **Answer**: In other words
   **Explanation**: "In other words" introduces a paraphrase or simpler restatement. Fixed phrase, comma after. Used to translate jargon, restate for clarity, or check shared understanding. Most common reformulation marker in spoken and written English.

2. **multiple_choice** — "The hypothesis predicts a non-linear relationship — _____, things won't scale evenly."
   **Options**: that is to say / that is for say / which is to say / that to say is
   **Answer**: that is to say
   **Explanation**: "That is to say" — slightly more formal than "in other words". Often written with comma both sides ("...non-linear relationship — that is to say, things..."). Used in academic and formal contexts.

3. **fill_gap** — "She's not just busy. _____, she's overwhelmed."
   **Hint**: Correction marker
   **Answer**: Or rather
   **Explanation**: "Or rather" introduces a self-correction — replacing a less-accurate term with a more-accurate one. Comma after. Used when you realise mid-sentence that you've understated or misstated something.

4. **fix_error** — "The data suggests, in other words, a downward trend."
   **Hint**: "In other words" doesn't fit mid-clause when there's no paraphrase
   **Answer**: The data suggests a downward trend. In other words, sales are slipping.
   **Explanation**: "In other words" introduces a restatement of a prior idea. It needs something to restate. Inserting it without a paraphrase makes it filler. Use only when you're actually paraphrasing.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The lecturer's gift was reformulation. 'The Heisenberg principle states that conjugate variables cannot be simultaneously measured to arbitrary precision. In other words, you can know where a particle is, or how fast it's moving — but never both at once. That is to say, observation imposes its own limits on what can be known. Or rather, observation IS the limit.'"
   **Question**: What is the lecturer's final, sharpest restatement?
   **Answer**: observation IS the limit
   **Explanation**: Three reformulations, each tighter than the last: "In other words..." (everyday paraphrase), "That is to say..." (philosophical implication), "Or rather..." (self-correction towards greater precision). Each marker does different work in a chain.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"In other words, sales are slipping."** — everyday paraphrase
2. **"That is to say, the conclusion follows."** — formal restatement
3. **"To put it another way, it's a trade-off."** — alternative phrasing
4. **"Or rather, it's more complicated than that."** — self-correction
5. **"By which I mean, the assumption was wrong."** — clarification

## Unit A04 — Above all, what's more

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Emphatic chain markers (above all, not least, what's more, for that matter, on top of that) — building up emphasis
> **Markers**: [denso] [discourse]
> **Real-life context**: You stack reasons or qualities — making the final element feel like the climax, not just another bullet point.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "She's smart, organised, and — _____ — she actually cares."
   **Options**: above all / above the all / on top all / over all
   **Answer**: above all
   **Explanation**: "Above all" — highest emphasis in a list. Fixed phrase, often set off by dashes or commas. Used to signal the most important item, typically saved for last. C1 register prefers this over "most importantly", which is fine but more pedestrian.

2. **multiple_choice** — "The product is well-designed. _____, the price is unbeatable."
   **Options**: What's more / What more / What's most / What is the more
   **Answer**: What's more
   **Explanation**: "What's / What is more" introduces an additional point that strengthens the case. Less climactic than "above all" — used mid-list to keep escalating. Fixed contraction with apostrophe.

3. **fill_gap** — "He's a great teacher — _____, the students adore him."
   **Hint**: "Not least"
   **Answer**: not least
   **Explanation**: "Not least" introduces a specifically important point, often understated. Fixed phrase. Means "and not at all unimportantly". Often introduces the most concrete or compelling evidence in a list.

4. **fix_error** — "Above all of, she's reliable."
   **Hint**: "Above all" is fixed — no "of" follows
   **Answer**: Above all, she's reliable.
   **Explanation**: "Above all" doesn't take a preposition. It's a standalone phrase. Common confusion with "above all of these" (where "of" links to a list). When used as a discourse marker at the start of a clause, just "Above all,".

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The job offer was strong on every front. The pay was generous. What's more, the team was small and experienced. Not least, the office was a fifteen-minute walk from her flat. Above all, she would finally be working for someone she respected without reservation. She had said yes before the call ended."
   **Question**: According to the passage, what was the most important factor in her decision?
   **Answer**: she would finally be working for someone she respected
   **Explanation**: Four emphatic markers building a climax: "What's / What is more" (adding), "Not least" (emphasising), "Above all" (final crown). Each marker escalates the emphasis. The marker "above all" signals the most important item.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Above all, she's honest."** — final emphasis
2. **"What's more, the price is fair."** — additional strength
3. **"Not least, the team trusts him."** — specifically important
4. **"On top of that, delivery is free."** — informal addition
5. **"For that matter, the timing is perfect too."** — extending the argument

## Unit A05 — All things considered, hence

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Conclusion markers (all things considered, on balance, on the whole, when all is said and done) + formal causal (hence, thereby, accordingly, by virtue of)
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You close arguments with weighted conclusions, or draw formal causal links in writing.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "_____, it was the right call."
   **Options**: All things considered / All things considering / All things being / Considering all things
   **Answer**: All things considered
   **Explanation**: "All things considered" — passive participle phrase used as a conclusion marker. Fixed form: "All things CONSIDERED", not "considering". Used to signal a weighted judgement after weighing factors. Comma after.

2. **multiple_choice** — "_____, the year exceeded expectations."
   **Options**: On the whole / In the whole / On a whole / By the whole
   **Answer**: On the whole
   **Explanation**: "On the whole" introduces an overall verdict. Synonyms: "all in all", "by and large", "overall". Fixed prepositional phrase. Used in writing and speech for balanced summary judgement.

3. **fill_gap** — "The contract was breached; _____, we are entitled to compensation."
   **Hint**: Formal causal marker
   **Answer**: hence
   **Explanation**: "Hence" = therefore, formal. Fixed marker introducing a consequence. Synonym: "accordingly", "thus". Common in legal, academic, business prose. Note: "hence" can also mean "from now" ("five years hence") but as discourse marker means "therefore".

4. **fix_error** — "On balance of, the decision was sound."
   **Hint**: "On balance" is fixed — no "of"
   **Answer**: On balance, the decision was sound.
   **Explanation**: "On balance" as a discourse marker doesn't take "of". The phrase "the balance of evidence" exists (a different construction) but "on balance" as conclusion marker stands alone with comma after.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The committee's report was thoughtful. 'On the whole, the project succeeded against difficult conditions. Granted, the budget overran by twelve percent. That said, the team delivered three weeks ahead of schedule. All things considered, the lessons learned outweigh the costs. Hence, we recommend continued investment in this direction.'"
   **Question**: What is the committee's final recommendation?
   **Answer**: continued investment in this direction
   **Explanation**: Five discourse markers in five sentences: "On the whole" (overall verdict), "Granted" (formal yield), "That said" (concession), "All things considered" (weighted conclusion), "Hence" (formal causal). The architecture of a balanced report.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"All things considered, it was the right call."** — weighted conclusion
2. **"On balance, the project was worth it."** — overall judgement
3. **"On the whole, the year went well."** — broad verdict
4. **"Hence, we recommend the new approach."** — formal causal
5. **"When all is said and done, results speak."** — final close

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M10, the student should:
- Sharpen claims with nuancing markers (in fact, as a matter of fact, actually, indeed)
- Concede gracefully (that said, having said that, mind you, then again, granted, admittedly)
- Reformulate clearly (in other words, that is to say, put differently, or rather, by which I mean)
- Stack emphasis (what's more, not least, above all, for that matter, on top of that)
- Close with weighted verdicts (all things considered, on balance, on the whole, when all is said and done)
- Use formal causal markers (hence, thereby, accordingly, by virtue of)
- Avoid stacking equivalent markers in the same clause
- Match marker register to surrounding prose

M11 (Conotação e registro) closes the C1 block — choosing words for their emotional and stylistic weight, not just denotation.
