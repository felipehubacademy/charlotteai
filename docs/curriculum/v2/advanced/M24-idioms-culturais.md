## Unit A01 — Idiomas com partes do corpo

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Body-part idioms — embodied metaphors deeply rooted in English
> **Markers**: [idiom] [cultural]
> **Real-life context**: You recognise and use body-based idioms in conversation, literature, and media.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "She wears her heart on her sleeve" means:
   **Options**: She shows emotions openly. / She's wearing fashionable clothes. / She loves shopping. / She's heartbroken.
   **Answer**: She shows emotions openly.
   **Explanation**: "Wear your heart on your sleeve" = display emotions openly, transparently. The image is mediaeval — knights wore tokens on their sleeves. The opposite is being guarded or stoic. Common in character descriptions.

2. **multiple_choice** — "After the breakup, he gave her the cold shoulder for months" means:
   **Options**: He deliberately ignored her. / He gave her a cold drink. / He hugged her warmly. / He turned his shoulder cold.
   **Answer**: He deliberately ignored her.
   **Explanation**: "Give the cold shoulder" = deliberately ignore someone, especially after a falling-out. Origin disputed (possibly mediaeval hospitality — serving guests a cold cut of shoulder when they overstayed). Now universal for social rejection.

3. **fill_gap** — "The renovation _____ an arm and a leg, but we love the result."
   **Hint**: idiom for "very expensive"
   **Answer**: cost
   **Explanation**: "Cost an arm and a leg" = extremely expensive. Origin debated (possibly from portrait painting where full-body portraits cost more). Used hyperbolically — never literally. Common in everyday speech.

4. **fix_error** — "Eu vou morder a bala e contar a verdade."
   **Hint**: Translate the idiomatic chunk
   **Answer**: I'm going to bite the bullet and tell the truth.
   **Explanation**: "Bite the bullet" = force yourself to do something unpleasant. Origin: pre-anaesthesia surgery, soldiers literally bit bullets to endure pain. Now used metaphorically for any difficult-but-necessary action. The Portuguese maps almost directly.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "She'd been planning to confront him for weeks, but every time she saw him, she lost her nerve. Finally, she bit the bullet over coffee on a Tuesday. He listened, then gave her the cold shoulder for the next three months. She, who normally wore her heart on her sleeve, learned to keep her feelings closer to her chest."
   **Question**: What three body-part idioms appear in the passage?
   **Answer**: "bit the bullet" (forced herself to act), "gave her the cold shoulder" (ignored her), "wore her heart on her sleeve" (showed emotions openly)
   **Explanation**: Three idioms layered: she bit the bullet (decisive action despite difficulty), he gave her the cold shoulder (deliberate rejection), she normally wore her heart on her sleeve (her usual emotional openness — now changed). Each idiom does specific narrative work.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"She wears her heart on her sleeve."** — emotional openness
2. **"He gave me the cold shoulder."** — deliberate ignoring
3. **"It cost an arm and a leg."** — extremely expensive
4. **"I'll bite the bullet and ask."** — force yourself
5. **"She's a real shoulder to cry on."** — empathetic support

## Unit A02 — Idiomas com animais

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Animal-based idioms — distinctly Anglo-cultural metaphors
> **Markers**: [idiom] [cultural]
> **Real-life context**: You use animal idioms naturally in conversation, recognising their idiomatic (not literal) meaning.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "You're barking up the wrong tree" means:
   **Options**: You're looking in the wrong place or asking the wrong person. / You're being loud like a dog. / You're climbing a tree wrong. / You're rude.
   **Answer**: You're looking in the wrong place or asking the wrong person.
   **Explanation**: "Bark up the wrong tree" = pursue a mistaken line of inquiry, accuse the wrong person, or look for something in the wrong place. Origin: hunting dogs barking at a tree where the prey has already left. Common in everyday English.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Sure, I'll lend him money — when pigs fly" means:
   **Options**: Never going to happen. / Soon. / Definitely. / When animals can fly.
   **Answer**: Never going to happen.
   **Explanation**: "When pigs fly" = never, since pigs cannot fly. Used sarcastically to refuse a request or express disbelief. Sister idioms: "When hell freezes over". Distinctly hyperbolic and humorous.

3. **fill_gap** — "She didn't mean to mention the party — she _____ the cat out of the bag."
   **Hint**: idiom for accidentally revealing
   **Answer**: let
   **Explanation**: "Let the cat out of the bag" = accidentally reveal a secret. Origin: mediaeval market trick — vendors selling "piglets" in bags that contained cats; opening the bag revealed the deception. Now means any inadvertent disclosure.

4. **fix_error** — "We have to talk about the elephant in the corner."
   **Hint**: The fixed idiom uses "room"
   **Answer**: We have to talk about the elephant in the room.
   **Explanation**: "The elephant in the room" = an obvious problem everyone is ignoring. Fixed idiom — "in the room" specifically, not "in the corner" or other variations. Common in difficult conversations and meetings.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The meeting started cordially, but everyone was avoiding the elephant in the room — the upcoming layoffs. When Maria finally let the cat out of the bag by mentioning specific names, the room went silent. The CEO had been barking up the wrong tree all morning, focusing on revenue when the real fear was jobs. Asked when announcements would come, he said 'soon' — which, knowing him, meant when pigs fly."
   **Question**: Which four idioms appear in the passage?
   **Answer**: "elephant in the room", "let the cat out of the bag", "barking up the wrong tree", "when pigs fly"
   **Explanation**: Four idioms threaded through the passage: the avoided topic (elephant), the accidental reveal (cat out of bag), the misguided focus (wrong tree), and the impossibility (pigs fly). Each does specific narrative work, replacing longer explanations with vivid images.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"You're barking up the wrong tree."** — looking in wrong place
2. **"When pigs fly."** — never
3. **"He let the cat out of the bag."** — accidental reveal
4. **"The elephant in the room is the budget."** — avoided topic
5. **"He spilled the beans about the party."** — revealed secret

## Unit A03 — Idiomas de tempo e raridade

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Time and frequency idioms — once in a blue moon, burn the midnight oil, out of the blue
> **Markers**: [idiom] [cultural]
> **Real-life context**: You describe rare events, late work, and sudden changes with time-based idioms.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "I see my cousin once in a blue moon" means:
   **Options**: Very rarely. / Every blue moon. / Once a month. / Often.
   **Answer**: Very rarely.
   **Explanation**: "Once in a blue moon" = very rarely. Origin: a blue moon is the second full moon in a calendar month, which happens roughly every 2-3 years. The metaphor extended to mean "extraordinarily infrequent". Universal idiom.

2. **multiple_choice** — "She was burning the midnight oil to finish the report" means:
   **Options**: Working very late. / Using oil at midnight. / Fighting a fire. / Burning candles.
   **Answer**: Working very late.
   **Explanation**: "Burn the midnight oil" = work late into the night, often on intensive tasks. Origin: pre-electricity, students and workers literally burned oil lamps to study/work after dark. Now used for any late work session.

3. **fill_gap** — "The job offer came _____ — I wasn't even looking."
   **Hint**: idiom for unexpected/sudden
   **Answer**: out of the blue
   **Explanation**: "Out of the blue" = unexpectedly, with no warning. Origin: refers to a clear blue sky from which a sudden bolt of lightning ("a bolt from the blue") would surprise everyone. Now used for any unexpected event.

4. **fix_error** — "Can we take a rain control?"
   **Hint**: The fixed phrase is "rain check"
   **Answer**: Can we take a rain check?
   **Explanation**: "Take a rain check" = postpone an invitation, with implicit promise to do it later. Origin: American baseball — when games were rained out, fans received a ticket ("rain check") for a future game. Common social phrase to decline politely.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Time flies when you're not paying attention. I hadn't seen Marco in years — we used to be close, but life moves and we lost touch. Then, out of the blue, he called last Tuesday. We met for coffee that same week. He was tired — he'd been burning the midnight oil on a startup that was either about to take off or crash, with no in-between. I told him to take a rain check on his other commitments and rest. He laughed, but the laugh was thin."
   **Question**: Which time-related idioms appear in the passage?
   **Answer**: "Time flies", "out of the blue", "burning the midnight oil", "take a rain check"
   **Explanation**: Four time idioms layered: time flies (time passes quickly), out of the blue (unexpectedly), burning the midnight oil (working late), take a rain check (postpone). Each compresses a longer concept into a vivid image.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"I see her once in a blue moon."** — very rarely
2. **"He's burning the midnight oil again."** — working late
3. **"It came completely out of the blue."** — unexpectedly
4. **"Can I take a rain check on dinner?"** — postpone politely
5. **"Time flies when you're busy."** — time passes quickly

## Unit A04 — Idiomas de decisão e ação

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Decision/action idioms — throw in the towel, ball is in your court, cutting corners, the last straw, back to square one
> **Markers**: [idiom] [cultural]
> **Real-life context**: You describe decisions, persistence, shortcuts, breakdowns, and restarts with action idioms.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "After six months of trying, he finally threw in the towel" means:
   **Options**: He gave up. / He did laundry. / He celebrated. / He started over.
   **Answer**: He gave up.
   **Explanation**: "Throw in the towel" = give up, admit defeat. Origin: boxing — a fighter's coach would throw a towel into the ring to signal surrender. Now used for any quitting or capitulation, especially after prolonged effort.

2. **multiple_choice** — "I've done my part — the ball is in your court now" means:
   **Options**: It's your turn to act. / We're playing tennis. / I have a sports ball. / You're winning.
   **Answer**: It's your turn to act.
   **Explanation**: "The ball is in your court" = it's your turn to make a move or decision. Origin: tennis — the ball must be returned by whichever side has it. Common in negotiations, projects, relationships.

3. **fill_gap** — "They're _____ corners on safety to save money — it'll backfire."
   **Hint**: idiom for skimping/taking shortcuts
   **Answer**: cutting
   **Explanation**: "Cutting corners" = taking shortcuts at the expense of quality or safety. Origin: literal — taking shorter paths instead of proper routes. Often used critically for compromises that create later problems.

4. **fix_error** — "That was the last drop — I'm quitting."
   **Hint**: The fixed idiom is "the last straw"

## Unit A05 — Idiomas conversacionais

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Conversational idioms — speak of the devil, piece of cake, break the ice, hit the nail on the head, rule of thumb
> **Markers**: [idiom] [cultural]
> **Real-life context**: You use everyday conversational idioms that signal native fluency.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Mid-conversation, Marco walks in just after being mentioned. You say:
   **Options**: Speak of the devil! / Look who's here! / Hi Marco. / Wow.
   **Answer**: Speak of the devil!
   **Explanation**: "Speak of the devil" = said when someone mentioned in conversation appears just then. Originally superstitious ("speak of the devil and he shall appear"), now humorous. Always used in surprise + recognition.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Breaking the ice with new colleagues" means:
   **Options**: Starting initial conversation. / Cooling drinks. / Cold weather chat. / Refusing to talk.
   **Answer**: Starting initial conversation.
   **Explanation**: "Break the ice" = initiate conversation in an awkward or formal setting, easing social tension. Origin: literal — icebreaker ships clear paths through ice. Now used metaphorically for any social opening move.

3. **fill_gap** — "She _____ the nail on the head — that's exactly the problem."
   **Hint**: idiom for being exactly right
   **Answer**: hit
   **Explanation**: "Hit the nail on the head" = identify precisely the right point or cause. Origin: hammering — striking the nail directly drives it in cleanly. Now used to praise accurate observations or diagnoses.

4. **fix_error** — "The exam was a piece of pizza."
   **Hint**: The fixed idiom is "piece of cake"
   **Answer**: The exam was a piece of cake.
   **Explanation**: "A piece of cake" = very easy. Origin disputed (possibly slavery-era cakewalk dances where prizes were cakes; or simply that eating cake is effortless). Always "cake" — never "pizza", "bread", or other substitutes.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "I was nervous about the meeting — I always struggle to break the ice with new clients. But Maria opened with a joke, and her first question hit the nail on the head: she asked the one thing nobody else had thought of. As a rule of thumb, I let her lead from there. Speak of the devil — she's just walked in. The presentation that took us a week to prepare turned out to be a piece of cake when she ran it."
   **Question**: Which five conversational idioms appear?
   **Answer**: "break the ice", "hit the nail on the head", "as a rule of thumb", "speak of the devil", "a piece of cake"
   **Explanation**: Five conversational idioms in one short passage: starting conversation (break the ice), being exactly right (hit the nail on the head), general principle (rule of thumb), coincidental appearance (speak of the devil), easy task (piece of cake). All high-frequency native conversational currency.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Speak of the devil!"** — when mentioned person appears
2. **"You hit the nail on the head."** — exactly right
3. **"It's a piece of cake."** — very easy
4. **"How do we break the ice?"** — start conversation
5. **"As a rule of thumb..."** — general principle

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M24, the student should:
- Use body-part idioms naturally (wear heart on sleeve, give cold shoulder, cost an arm and a leg, bite the bullet)
- Use animal idioms appropriately (barking up the wrong tree, when pigs fly, let the cat out of the bag, elephant in the room)
- Use time/rarity idioms (once in a blue moon, burn the midnight oil, out of the blue, rain check, time flies)
- Use action/decision idioms (throw in the towel, ball is in your court, cutting corners, the last straw, back to square one)
- Use conversational idioms (speak of the devil, piece of cake, break the ice, hit the nail on the head, rule of thumb)
- Recognize idiomatic vs literal meaning instantly
- Layer multiple idioms in extended discourse without sounding forced
- Match idiom register to context (casual, professional, literary)
