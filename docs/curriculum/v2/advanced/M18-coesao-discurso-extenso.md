# Module M18 — Cohesion in extended discourse

> **Level**: Advanced (C2)
> **Block**: C2 Block
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Reference chains, lexical cohesion (synonyms, hyponyms, repetition), old-information-first principle, bridging implicit links, avoiding ambiguous referents
> **Module goal**: Student writes extended discourse where every sentence connects clearly to the one before — through pronouns, lexical chains, and information flow.
> **Connects to**: M19 (Sutileza modal) — moves from discourse-level cohesion to modal nuance.

## Module chunks introduced (~25)

- this / that / these / those (demonstratives as reference)
- the former / the latter
- such X (summative reference)
- it / they / one (pronominal reference)
- the same / a similar / a related
- aforementioned / above-mentioned / the X in question (formal)
- old information at sentence start, new at end
- bridging via inference (no marker, but logical link)
- hyponym / superordinate chains (a Toyota → the car → the vehicle)
- synonymic chains for elegant variation
- this/that as topic-summariser

## Unit A01 — This, that as reference

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Demonstratives (this/that/these/those) as anaphoric reference to previous content
> **Markers**: [denso] [cohesion]
> **Real-life context**: You point back to what's just been said with the right demonstrative — keeping discourse threaded without repetition.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Sales fell 12% in Q3. _____ was the worst result in five years."
   **Options**: This / That / Those / Such
   **Answer**: This
   **Explanation**: "This" — anaphoric reference to the immediately preceding statement (the 12% fall). "This was the worst result" — "this" stands for the whole prior fact. "That" works too but feels slightly more distanced. "This" is the default for immediate reference.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Three executives have left in the past month. _____ raises serious questions."
   **Options**: This / That / These / Those
   **Answer**: This
   **Explanation**: "This + summative noun" works even for plural subjects in the prior clause. "Three executives have left... This raises questions" — "this" refers to the FACT or SITUATION, which is singular abstract. Compare "These executives have left, and they all said..." — there "these" + plural noun refers to the people themselves.

3. **fill_gap** — "The proposal was rejected for three reasons: cost, timing, and political risk. _____ three concerns shaped the rest of the discussion."
   **Hint**: "These" + the three reasons just listed
   **Answer**: These
   **Explanation**: "These three concerns" — "these" because the antecedent is plural ("three reasons"). The demonstrative gathers the just-listed items and names them with a summary noun ("concerns"). This pattern — "These X..." — is essential for extended discourse cohesion.

4. **fix_error** — "The team missed two deadlines. This deadlines worried the client."
   **Hint**: "This + plural noun" doesn't work; use "These"
   **Answer**: The team missed two deadlines. These deadlines worried the client.
   **Explanation**: "This" goes with singular nouns; "these" with plural. "This deadlines" mixes a singular demonstrative with a plural noun — agreement error. Common slip when reference moves quickly.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The report identified three systemic failures. The first was poor data hygiene. The second was unclear ownership. The third was a culture that punished bad news. These three failures, the report argued, were not independent. This made the recommended fixes harder to design — and easier to delay."
   **Question**: What did the report argue about the three failures?
   **Answer**: that they were not independent (they were interconnected)
   **Explanation**: Three demonstratives weaving the text: "The first / The second / The third" (ordinal sequence), "These three failures" (gathers them as plural), "This made..." (singular, referring back to the whole prior statement). Each demonstrative connects clearly to its referent.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"This raises serious questions."** — singular abstract reference
2. **"These concerns shaped the discussion."** — plural list reference
3. **"That was the worst result in years."** — distanced singular reference
4. **"Those decisions still haunt us."** — distanced plural reference
5. **"Such patterns rarely resolve themselves."** — formal summative reference

## Unit A02 — Lexical chains

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Lexical cohesion via synonyms, hyponyms, and superordinates (a Toyota → the car → the vehicle → the machine)
> **Markers**: [denso] [cohesion]
> **Real-life context**: You vary vocabulary while keeping the referent clear — moving from specific to general or substituting synonyms.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Cohesive chain: "She drove a 1972 Volvo. The car was barely roadworthy. _____ vehicle had been her father's pride."
   **Options**: The / This / That / Such
   **Answer**: The
   **Accepts**: The same
   **Explanation**: Lexical cohesion via superordinate hierarchy: "Volvo" (specific) → "car" (less specific) → "vehicle" (even less specific). Each refers to the same object. "The vehicle" — definite article + superordinate noun, picking up the referent without repetition. Hyponym → superordinate chain.

2. **multiple_choice** — Synonym substitution: "The proposal was bold. The plan would change the entire department. _____ would also test the team."
   **Options**: The initiative / The proposal / The plan / The thing
   **Answer**: The initiative
   **Explanation**: Synonym chain: "proposal" → "plan" → "initiative". Same referent, different word. Avoids monotonous repetition. Native writers naturally cycle through synonyms when the referent persists across sentences.

3. **fill_gap** — Lexical chain: "She rescued a stray dog last winter. _____ animal had been on the street for weeks."
   **Hint**: Superordinate of "dog"
   **Answer**: The (animal)
   **Explanation**: "The animal" — moves up from specific ("dog") to superordinate ("animal"). The article "the" signals shared knowledge. This is cohesion through hyponym → superordinate, sustained by definite article.

4. **fix_error** — "He bought a Tesla. He bought a Tesla. He bought a Tesla last year, and a Tesla is expensive to maintain."
   **Hint**: Vary with synonyms / superordinates
   **Answer**: He bought a Tesla last year. The car has been expensive to maintain — the technology is unforgiving when something breaks.
   **Explanation**: Triple repetition of "Tesla" is heavy. Cohesion through variation: Tesla → the car → the technology. Each refers to the same purchase, but the prose breathes.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "She had brought home a rescue puppy in March. The dog was nervous of everyone, especially men. The little creature would not let anyone but her near it for three weeks. By June, the animal had begun to trust the postman. By autumn, the once-fearful pup followed her into every room."
   **Question**: How does the passage avoid repeating 'puppy' / 'dog'?
   **Answer**: through a lexical chain — puppy → the dog → the little creature → the animal → the once-fearful pup
   **Explanation**: Five lexical variants for the same referent: rescue puppy, the dog, the little creature, the animal, the once-fearful pup. Each adds a slight nuance (age, size, fear, recovery). Cohesion via varied lexicon — typical of C2 narrative.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The car had been her father's pride."** — superordinate cohesion
2. **"The initiative would test the team."** — synonym substitution
3. **"The animal trusted no one but her."** — hyponym → superordinate
4. **"The proposal — the plan — the project."** — synonym cycling
5. **"The technology was unforgiving."** — variant after specific name

## Unit A03 — The former, the latter, such

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Formal anaphoric devices — the former / the latter, such X, the aforementioned, the X in question
> **Markers**: [denso] [cohesion]
> **Real-life context**: You write formal prose where you need to refer back unambiguously to one of two prior items, or summarise prior text.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "We considered both Paris and Lisbon. The former offers more nightlife; _____ offers more affordability."
   **Options**: the latter / the late / the laster / the second
   **Answer**: the latter
   **Explanation**: "The former / The latter" — formal devices for referring to first and second of two previously mentioned items. "The former" = the first mentioned (Paris); "the latter" = the second mentioned (Lisbon). Common in formal writing and comparison.

2. **multiple_choice** — "_____ disruptions are increasingly common in global supply chains."
   **Options**: Such / These such / Such of / Such kind of
   **Answer**: Such
   **Explanation**: "Such + noun" — summative reference to previously characterised entities. "Such disruptions" = "the kind of disruptions just described". Common in academic and policy prose. Formal alternative to "these kinds of" or "this type of".

3. **fill_gap** — Formal: "The aforementioned report concluded that the policy had failed. _____ findings remain unchallenged."
   **Hint**: Synonymous formal demonstrative
   **Answer**: These
   **Accepts**: The aforementioned
   **Explanation**: "These findings" — anaphoric link to the report's content just mentioned. Alternatively, formal: "The aforementioned findings". "Aforementioned" itself is highly formal and slightly archaic — used in legal and very formal academic writing.

4. **fix_error** — "We had two options: A and B. I prefer the formal."
   **Hint**: "The former" is the term, not "the formal"
   **Answer**: We had two options: A and B. I prefer the former.
   **Explanation**: "The formal" means "the formal one" (relating to formality). "The former" means "the first of two previously mentioned". Common L2 confusion — easy to slip the wrong word into formal English. C2 watchfulness.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The committee narrowed the field to two candidates: Patel and Müller. The former had more international experience; the latter had built a stronger team in the past five years. Such backgrounds, the committee agreed, would suit different strategic moments. The candidate in question, after further interviews, would be Müller."
   **Question**: Who did the committee ultimately choose?
   **Answer**: Müller (the latter)
   **Explanation**: Four formal anaphoric devices: "The former" (Patel), "the latter" (Müller), "Such backgrounds" (summative), "The candidate in question" (formal definite reference). Each does specific anaphoric work in formal prose.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The former offers more nightlife."** — first of two
2. **"The latter offers more affordability."** — second of two
3. **"Such disruptions are common now."** — summative reference
4. **"The aforementioned report concluded..."** — formal back-reference
5. **"The candidate in question withdrew."** — specific formal reference

## Unit A04 — Old information first

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Information flow — placing old/given information at the start of a sentence and new information at the end (theme-rheme structure)
> **Markers**: [denso] [discourse]
> **Real-life context**: You write extended prose where each sentence begins with what's already known and ends with new information — building cumulatively.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Better information flow: "We had a problem with the deadline. _____."
   **Options**: This problem stemmed from poor estimates. / Poor estimates were the source of this problem. / Estimates were what poor were the source.
   **Answer**: This problem stemmed from poor estimates.
   **Explanation**: "This problem" (old, already mentioned) → "stemmed from poor estimates" (new information). Standard theme-rheme structure: known information at sentence-start, new at sentence-end. Reader's mind builds on what they already know.

2. **multiple_choice** — Better information flow: "The new hire impressed everyone. _____."
   **Options**: His confidence in meetings was particularly striking. / Particularly striking was his confidence in meetings. / Confidence in meetings was particularly his striking.
   **Answer**: His confidence in meetings was particularly striking.
   **Explanation**: "His" (referring back to the new hire = old info) → "confidence... particularly striking" (new info). Old information anchors; new information lands. Reader experiences a forward motion: known → new.

3. **fill_gap** — Information flow: "We discussed the budget. _____ revealed significant risks."
   **Hint**: Reference back with old info first
   **Answer**: That discussion
   **Accepts**: The discussion
   **Explanation**: "That discussion" (referring back) → "revealed significant risks" (new info). Or "The discussion" — definite article carries old info. The sentence builds on what was just mentioned.

4. **fix_error** — Reverse information flow: "Significant risks were revealed by our discussion of the budget."
   **Hint**: Lead with old info ("our discussion") and end with new ("significant risks")
   **Answer**: Our discussion of the budget revealed significant risks.
   **Explanation**: The passive version inverts the natural information flow — new info ("significant risks") at the start, old info ("our discussion") at the end. Awkward unless the passive serves a specific purpose. Active version aligns with theme-rheme.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The committee made three decisions in its first hour. The first was to extend the deadline. This extension would relieve the most acute pressure on the team. The second was to bring in an external auditor. That auditor would arrive on Monday. The third decision was the hardest: to reassign the lead engineer."
   **Question**: How does each sentence begin?
   **Answer**: with old information referring back to what was just said (the first / This extension / The second / That auditor / The third decision)
   **Explanation**: Every sentence begins with an old-information anchor referring back: "The first" / "This extension" / "The second" / "That auditor" / "The third decision". Each closes with new information. Information flow is invisible because it works.

## Unit A05 — Bridging and avoiding ambiguous referents

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Bridging (implied connections without explicit markers) and avoiding ambiguous "it / this / they" references
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You write coherently even without explicit connectives — relying on inference — while making sure every pronoun has a clear antecedent.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Bridging without explicit marker: "The car wouldn't start. _____ was completely dead."
   **Options**: The battery / The car / The engine / The starter
   **Answer**: The battery (or any car part — bridging via inference)
   **Explanation**: Bridging cohesion: the reader infers that "the battery" belongs to "the car" without it being stated. Native readers fill the gap. The definite article "the" signals shared knowledge. Common in fluent prose — no "the car's battery", just "the battery".

2. **multiple_choice** — Ambiguous: "Maria told her sister that she would arrive late." Who would arrive late?
   **Options**: Maria / Her sister / Unclear / Both
   **Answer**: Unclear
   **Explanation**: "She" is ambiguous — could refer to Maria or her sister. C2 writing fixes this. Clearer: "Maria told her sister that Maria would arrive late" (repetition) or "Maria told her sister, 'I'll / I will be late.'" (direct speech) or "Maria warned her sister of her own late arrival." (rephrase).

3. **fill_gap** — Disambiguate: "When Maria saw her colleague at the conference, she was already heading to the door. To be clear: _____ was heading to the door."
   **Hint**: Specify Maria or the colleague
   **Answer**: Maria (or "the colleague" — whichever the writer intended)
   **Explanation**: When pronouns risk ambiguity, name the referent. Repetition of the name is preferable to confusion. C2 writers do this — explicit > elegant when stakes are high.

4. **fix_error** — Ambiguous reference: "I gave the report to my manager, but it was incomplete."
   **Hint**: What was incomplete — the report or the manager?
   **Answer**: I gave the report to my manager, but the report was incomplete.
   **Accepts**: I gave my manager the report, which was still incomplete.
   **Explanation**: "It" could refer to the report or the manager. Repeat the noun to disambiguate, or restructure the sentence. C2 prose avoids reference ambiguity even at the cost of slight repetition.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "She walked into the room and turned on the light. The bulb flickered, hesitated, then steadied. Books lined three walls. The shelves were her grandfather's — every piece of wood pulled from a barn he had grown up in. She did not touch them yet. The room had a way of letting itself be entered slowly."
   **Question**: How does the passage establish cohesion without explicit connectives?
   **Answer**: through bridging — "the light" implies "in the room", "the bulb" implies "the light", "the shelves" implies "the walls", "the room" returns to the initial subject. Each definite article signals shared knowledge built from the previous sentence.
   **Explanation**: Six bridges: "the room" → "the light" → "the bulb" → "the walls" → "the shelves" → "the room". No "however" or "therefore" — just shared knowledge of the scene. C2 prose at its most fluent.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The battery was dead."** — bridging from "car"
2. **"Maria warned her sister she'd be late."** — disambiguated pronoun
3. **"The report was incomplete."** — repeat noun to clarify

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M18, the student should:
- Use this / that / these / those for anaphoric reference, matching number to antecedent
- Form lexical chains (Toyota → the car → the vehicle → the machine)
- Vary synonyms across sentences (proposal → plan → initiative)
- Use formal anaphoric devices (the former / the latter, such X, the aforementioned, the X in question)
- Place old information at sentence start, new information at sentence end (theme-rheme)
- Bridge between sentences via definite articles and inferable connections (the room → the light → the bulb)
- Disambiguate pronouns by naming the referent when "it / she / they" risks confusion
- Keep readers oriented across long discourse without heavy connective load

M19 (Sutileza modal) takes cohesion into modal nuance — the fine shades of certainty, obligation, and possibility.
