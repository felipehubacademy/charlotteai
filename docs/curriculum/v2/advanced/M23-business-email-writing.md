# Module M23 — Business & email writing

> **Level**: Advanced (C1)
> **Block**: C1 extension (Fase B)
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Professional written English — formal emails, job applications, cover letters, follow-ups, workplace messaging
> **Module goal**: Student writes professional emails, applications, follow-ups and workplace messages with appropriate register, hedging, and clarity.
> **Connects to**: A12 (academic writing) covers academic prose; A23 is workplace-specific. Pairs with A08 (hedging), A05 (passive), A10 (discourse markers).

## Module chunks introduced (~28)

- Subject lines: "Following up on...", "Re: [topic]", "Quick question about..."
- Formal openings: "I hope this email finds you well", "I'm reaching out regarding..."
- Semi-formal openings: "Hope you're doing well", "Quick note about..."
- Body chunks: "I'd like to...", "I'd appreciate it if...", "Would it be possible to..."
- Cover letter chunks: "I am writing to apply for...", "I bring [X years] of experience in..."
- Follow-up chunks: "Just following up on...", "Wanted to check in on...", "Any update on...?"
- Hedging: "It might be worth considering...", "Perhaps we could...", "I'd suggest..."
- Closings: "Best regards", "Kind regards", "Best", "Thanks again"
- Bad news softening: "Unfortunately...", "I regret to inform you..."
- Apologizing: "Apologies for the delay...", "I appreciate your patience"

## Unit A01 — Email openings & closings

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Email openings and closings — matching register to relationship and purpose (formal, semi-formal, casual professional) | **Tense**: REGISTER · business
> **Markers**: [register]
> **Real-life context**: You open and close emails appropriately — neither too stiff for a coworker nor too casual for a client.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Most formal opening (cold email to senior executive):
   **Options**: I hope this email finds you well. / Hi there! / Hey, what's up? / Hello.
   **Answer**: I hope this email finds you well.
   **Explanation**: "I hope this email finds you well" — quintessential formal email opener for first contact with senior or unknown recipients. Slightly clichéd but universally accepted. "Hi there!" and "Hey, what's / what is up?" are too casual for formal contexts.

2. **multiple_choice** — Most appropriate closing for a formal cover letter:
   **Options**: Kind regards / Best / Cheers / Talk soon
   **Answer**: Kind regards
   **Explanation**: "Kind regards" — formal closing for cover letters, applications, and emails to people you don't know well. "Best regards" is also fine. "Best" alone is semi-formal. "Cheers" is informal British. "Talk soon" is casual.

3. **fill_gap** — Semi-formal opener for a coworker: "_____ you're doing well."
   **Hint**: friendly but professional opener
   **Answer**: Hope
   **Accepts**: Hoping
   **Explanation**: "Hope you're / you are doing well" — common semi-formal opener for someone you know but aren't close to. Drops the formal "I" of "I hope" to feel warmer. Widely used in workplace email.

4. **fix_error** — Cold email opening: "Hey there, what's going on?"
   **Hint**: Too casual for a cold business email
   **Answer**: Dear [Name], I hope this email finds you well. I'm reaching out regarding...
   **Explanation**: "Hey there, what's / what is going on?" is appropriate among friends but jarring in business cold-outreach. Cold emails need formal openings: "Dear [Name]" + "I hope this email finds you well" or "I'm / I am reaching out regarding...". Register match is essential.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Dear Ms. Silva, I hope this email finds you well. I'm reaching out regarding the position you advertised last week. Please find my CV attached for your consideration. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my experience aligns with your team's needs. Kind regards, Maria."
   **Question**: Identify the formal opener and the formal closer used.
   **Answer**: opener: "I hope this email finds you well"; closer: "Kind regards"
   **Explanation**: The email uses two formal anchors: "Dear Ms. Silva" + "I hope this email finds you well" at the opening; "Kind regards" at the closing. Both signal high register suitable for job applications.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"I hope this email finds you well."** — quintessential formal opener
2. **"Hope you're doing well."** — semi-formal warm opener
3. **"I'm reaching out regarding..."** — purpose-stating opener
4. **"Best regards / Kind regards."** — formal closings
5. **"Thanks again, talk soon."** — casual professional closer

## Unit A02 — Job applications

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Job application email body — opening line, why-me paragraph, role fit, closing line | **Tense**: REGISTER · business
> **Markers**: [register]
> **Real-life context**: You write an application email that gets read, demonstrates fit, and ends with a clear ask for the next step.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Most professional opening for a job application:
   **Options**: I am writing to apply for the position of [role]. / Hey, I want the job. / Hi! I saw the ad. / I would like to be hired.
   **Answer**: I am writing to apply for the position of [role].
   **Explanation**: "I am writing to apply for the position of [role] advertised on [source]" is the standard professional formula. Direct, clear, and signals competence. The other options are either too casual or grammatically awkward.

2. **multiple_choice** — Strongest closing line for an application:
   **Options**: I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my background aligns with your team's needs. / I really want this job. / Please call me. / Hope to hear from you.
   **Answer**: I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my background aligns with your team's needs.
   **Explanation**: This closing demonstrates: professional register ("I would welcome"), specific value framing ("how my background aligns"), and a soft ask for next step. The others lack specificity or sound desperate ("I really want this job").

3. **fill_gap** — Application body chunk: "I bring _____ years of experience in [field], with a track record of..."
   **Hint**: number of years (template)
   **Answer**: [number] (e.g., "five" or "ten")
   **Explanation**: "I bring [X] years of experience in [field], with a track record of [specific results]" — formula for quantifying experience with credibility. Always replace [X] with the actual number; "many" or "lots of" weakens the claim.

4. **fix_error** — Application opening: "Hello, I want to apply to your company for the marketing job."
   **Hint**: Too casual for a formal application
   **Answer**: I am writing to apply for the Marketing position advertised on [LinkedIn / your website].
   **Explanation**: "Hello, I want to" is conversational; applications need formal phrasing: "I am writing to apply for [Position Name] advertised on [source]". Capitalize the position name; specify the source for tracking.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Dear Hiring Manager, I am writing to apply for the Senior Designer position advertised on LinkedIn. I bring eight years of experience in product design, with a track record of leading design systems at scale. In my current role at [Company], I have shipped three major product launches that drove a 30% increase in user engagement. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my background aligns with your team's needs. Kind regards, Maria."
   **Question**: What three application elements are present in this paragraph?
   **Answer**: (1) formal opening with position name + source; (2) quantified experience + track record; (3) specific results + closing ask
   **Explanation**: A complete application body has three pillars: clear opening (formal + specific position + source), credibility (years + track record + specific results), and closing (welcome opportunity + soft ask). This template scales across roles and industries.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"I am writing to apply for the position of [role]."** — formal opener
2. **"I bring [X] years of experience in [field]."** — quantified credibility
3. **"In my current role, I have [specific achievement]."** — track record
4. **"My background aligns with your team's needs."** — fit statement
5. **"I would welcome the opportunity to discuss."** — closing ask

## Unit A03 — Cover letters & CVs language

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Cover letter language and CV bullet points — strong action verbs, quantification, achievement framing | **Tense**: REGISTER · business
> **Markers**: [register]
> **Real-life context**: You craft cover letter paragraphs and CV bullets that highlight achievements with concrete impact.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Strongest CV bullet:
   **Options**: Led cross-functional team of 12 to deliver a $2M product launch ahead of schedule. / Worked on a project. / Helped with stuff. / Did some leading.
   **Answer**: Led cross-functional team of 12 to deliver a $2M product launch ahead of schedule.
   **Explanation**: Strong CV bullet = action verb (Led) + scope (12 people) + outcome ($2M product launch) + qualifier (ahead of schedule). The others are vague and don't communicate value. Quantify everything possible.

2. **multiple_choice** — Best action verb for "I was responsible for":
   **Options**: Spearheaded / Was given / Got assigned / Had to do
   **Answer**: Spearheaded
   **Explanation**: "Spearheaded" = strong action verb showing initiative and ownership. Other options sound passive or weak. CV verbs to favour: Led, Spearheaded, Drove, Built, Launched, Scaled, Optimised, Restructured, Negotiated.

3. **fill_gap** — Cover letter chunk: "What draws me to [Company] is _____."
   **Hint**: Specific reason connecting your interests to the company
   **Answer**: [specific reason — e.g., "your commitment to sustainable design"]
   **Explanation**: "What draws me to [Company] is..." is a strong cover letter pivot showing you've researched the company. Always follow with a specific company value or initiative — generic "your innovative culture" reads as filler.

4. **fix_error** — CV bullet: "Was the manager of a team that did some stuff."
   **Hint**: Use a strong action verb + quantification
   **Answer**: Managed a team of [X] to deliver [specific outcome] resulting in [quantified impact].
   **Explanation**: Strong CV bullets follow the formula: [Strong action verb] + [scope/team] + [concrete deliverable] + [quantified impact]. "Did some stuff" is the antithesis of professional CV writing.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "What draws me to ClimateLab is your commitment to ocean restoration at scale — I've followed your work since the 2022 reef project. In my five years at Aquatic Research, I led the design of three monitoring platforms that are now deployed across 12 coastal sites. The technical challenge of building for hostile environments — combined with the urgency of the mission — is exactly the work I want to do for the next decade."
   **Question**: What three elements make this cover letter paragraph strong?
   **Answer**: (1) specific company research ("ocean restoration", "2022 reef project"); (2) quantified track record ("three monitoring platforms", "12 coastal sites"); (3) personal motivation tied to mission
   **Explanation**: Strong cover letter paragraphs do three things: show specific research (you know what they do), establish credibility (concrete achievements with numbers), and connect personal motivation to their mission. Each sentence does work.

3. **"What draws me to [Company] is..."** — cover letter pivot

## Unit A04 — Follow-ups & check-ins

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Follow-up emails after silence, post-meeting follow-ups, application follow-ups — gentle persistence without pushiness | **Tense**: REGISTER · business

1. **multiple_choice** — Best follow-up opener after a week of silence:
   **Options**: Just following up on my email below — wondering if you've had a chance to review. / Why didn't you reply? / Did you get my email? / Are you ignoring me?
   **Answer**: Just following up on my email below — wondering if you've had a chance to review.
   **Explanation**: "Just following up..." is the gold standard — polite, persistent, gives them an easy out ("had a chance to review"). The accusatory or anxious options damage relationships.

2. **multiple_choice** — Most professional check-in after a meeting:
   **Options**: Following up on our conversation — happy to share more detail on [topic] if useful. / Hey, did we agree on something? / Just wanted to make sure you remember me. / Are we still doing this?

   **Explanation**: "Did you read my email or what?" reads as hostile. Always soften with "just following up", "just checking in", or "wondering if you had a chance to review". The phrase "if you had a chance" acknowledges their time.

## Unit A05 — Difficult emails

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Declining politely, delivering bad news, asking for raises/promotions, apologizing professionally | **Tense**: REGISTER · business
> **Markers**: [register]
> **Real-life context**: You handle the hardest emails — saying no, sharing bad news, advocating for yourself — with professional grace.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Most professional decline:
   **Options**: Thank you for thinking of me. Unfortunately, this isn't a fit for my current focus, but I'd be happy to recommend others. / No, I'm not interested. / I don't want to. / Maybe another time.
   **Answer**: Thank you for thinking of me. Unfortunately, this isn't a fit for my current focus, but I'd be happy to recommend others.
   **Explanation**: Professional declines combine gratitude ("thank you"), reason ("isn't / is not a fit for my current focus"), and offer of value ("happy to recommend others"). The others damage relationships or sound abrupt.

2. **multiple_choice** — Strongest framing for asking for a raise:
   **Options**: I'd like to discuss compensation given my expanded role and impact over the past 18 months. / I want more money. / Give me a raise please. / I deserve more pay.
   **Answer**: I'd like to discuss compensation given my expanded role and impact over the past 18 months.
   **Explanation**: Compensation conversations need: clear framing ("discuss compensation"), specific reasoning ("expanded role"), time anchor ("past 18 months"). "I want more money" or "I deserve" reads as demanding without justification.

3. **fill_gap** — Bad news softener: "_____ , we won't be able to extend an offer at this time."
   **Hint**: Standard bad news opener
   **Answer**: Unfortunately
   **Explanation**: "Unfortunately" — the standard bad news softener. Universal across formal contexts. Variants: "I regret to inform you that...", "Sadly...". Always pair with reason or path forward when possible.

4. **fix_error** — Decline email: "No, I won't be coming to the meeting. I'm busy."
   **Hint**: Too curt — soften with apology + alternative
   **Answer**: Unfortunately, I won't be able to attend the meeting on Friday — I have a competing commitment. Happy to follow up on the action items afterward.
   **Explanation**: Professional declines soften the no ("Unfortunately"), give a brief reason ("competing commitment"), and offer engagement ("happy to follow up"). "I'm / I am busy" sounds dismissive.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Hi João, Thanks for sending the proposal — I appreciate the work that went into it. Unfortunately, the budget for this project isn't going to allow us to move forward at the scope you've outlined. I'd love to keep the door open for a scaled-down version, or a future collaboration when budgets reset in Q1. Apologies for any inconvenience, and thanks again for putting it together. Best, Maria."
   **Question**: What three professional moves does this 'no' make?
   **Answer**: (1) gratitude/acknowledgment ("thanks for sending", "appreciate the work"); (2) clear reason ("budget isn't going to allow"); (3) keeps door open ("scaled-down version, or a future collaboration")
   **Explanation**: Strong declines preserve relationships. Three moves: acknowledge the effort, give a real reason, leave a door open. Never just say no — always frame the no with grace and a path forward.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"Unfortunately, this isn't a fit at this time."** — professional decline
