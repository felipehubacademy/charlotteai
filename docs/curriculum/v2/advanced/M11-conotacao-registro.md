> **Level**: Advanced (C1)
> **Block**: C1 Block (closing)
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Word connotation (positive/negative/neutral), formal vs informal register, euphemism, loaded language, litotes and hyperbole, code-switching

- careful / cautious / hesitant / timid

## Unit A01 — Assertive or pushy?

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Synonyms with different connotations — choosing the word that signals your judgement
> **Markers**: [denso] [connotation]
> **Real-life context**: You describe the same behaviour positively, neutrally, or negatively depending on what you actually think.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "I admire her — she's very _____ in meetings."
   **Options**: assertive / aggressive / pushy / domineering
   **Answer**: assertive
   **Explanation**: "Assertive" is positive — confident, clear, appropriate. "Aggressive" carries negative weight (hostile, combative). "Pushy" is mild-negative (annoyingly persistent). "Domineering" is strongly negative (controlling). Same behaviour, different verdicts. C1 fluency lives in this choice.

2. **multiple_choice** — "He's incredibly _____ — he'll do anything to win."
   **Options**: ruthless / ambitious / driven / determined
   **Answer**: ruthless
   **Explanation**: "Ruthless" is negative-loaded (willing to harm). "Ambitious" is mostly positive. "Driven" is neutral-to-positive (intensely motivated). "Determined" is positive (committed). The "do anything" context signals negative connotation, so "ruthless" fits.

3. **fill_gap** — "She's not stingy with the budget — she's _____."
   **Hint**: positive word for careful with money
   **Answer**: thrifty (or "frugal")
   **Explanation**: "Stingy" is strongly negative (selfishly cheap). "Thrifty" is positive (wisely economical). "Frugal" is neutral-positive (austere by choice). "Cheap" sits negative when applied to a person. Reframing "stingy" as "thrifty" reverses the moral charge while describing the same act.

4. **fix_error** — "Your essay was great — really skinny and direct."
   **Hint**: "Skinny" is mildly negative for body, wrong word for prose
   **Answer**: Your essay was great — really lean and direct. (or: tight, concise)
   **Explanation**: "Skinny" carries unkind body-image weight. Applied to writing, the better word is "lean" or "tight" or "concise" — all positive. Connotation matters: "slim" leans neutral, "slender" leans positive, "skinny/scrawny" lean negative.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The two profiles painted opposite pictures from the same facts. One described her as confident, assertive, and ambitious; the other called her cocky, aggressive, and ruthless. Both writers, the reporter realised, had spent the same hour with her. Only the connotations differed."
   **Question**: What did the reporter realise about the two profiles?
   **Answer**: they used different connotations to describe the same facts (or "only the connotations differed")
   **Explanation**: Three contrasting pairs: "confident vs cocky", "assertive vs aggressive", "ambitious vs ruthless". Each pair denotes similar behaviour but encodes a different judgement. Word choice is moral judgement compressed into a syllable.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"She's assertive, not aggressive."** — positive reframe
2. **"He's ambitious — driven, not ruthless."** — positive nuance
3. **"I'd call her thrifty, not stingy."** — defending the trait
4. **"He's confident, bordering on cocky."** — naming the line
5. **"She's careful, not timid."** — distinguishing trait from flaw

## Unit A02 — Commence, ascertain, demonstrate

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Formal vs informal register pairs (start/commence, ask/inquire, find out/ascertain, get/obtain, show/demonstrate, put off/postpone, look into/investigate)
> **Markers**: [denso] [register]
> **Real-life context**: You shift register up for formal writing (academic, legal, business) or down for natural speech — knowing both halves of each pair.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Formal register: "We will _____ the audit on Monday."
   **Options**: commence / start / kick off / fire up
   **Answer**: commence
   **Explanation**: "Commence" — formal Latinate verb, used in legal/business/ceremonial contexts. "Start" is the neutral default. "Kick off" is informal. "Fire up" is colloquial. C1 register requires matching word formality to context. A formal audit notice uses "commence".

2. **multiple_choice** — Formal: "Please _____ as to the status of the order."
   **Options**: inquire / ask / find out / check
   **Answer**: inquire
   **Explanation**: "Inquire" (or British "enquire") — formal register for asking. Common in customer service templates and formal correspondence. "Ask" is the neutral verb. "Find out" is informal. Mismatched register signals carelessness in formal contexts.

3. **fill_gap** — Formal: "The investigation will _____ whether the claim is valid."
   **Hint**: Formal verb for "find out"
   **Answer**: ascertain
   **Explanation**: "Ascertain" — formal Latinate verb meaning to find out for certain. Common in legal, investigative, academic writing. Informal counterpart: "find out" or "check". Register placement matters.

4. **fix_error** — Formal email: "We need to kick off the project."
   **Hint**: For formal context, use "commence" instead
   **Answer**: We need to commence the project.
   **Explanation**: "Kick off" is sports-informal, common in casual business speech but jarring in a formal email. The register-correct version uses "commence" or "begin" or "initiate". C1 awareness of register includes detecting these mismatches.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The letter was a study in formal register. 'The committee has resolved to commence proceedings. We have ascertained that the documents in question were obtained without authorisation. We will demonstrate, in due course, that the breach was deliberate. Should you wish to inquire further, our office remains available at the address below.'"
   **Question**: What does the letter promise the committee will do "in due course"?
   **Answer**: demonstrate that the breach was deliberate
   **Explanation**: Five formal-register verbs in five sentences: "commence" (formal "start"), "ascertained" (formal "found out"), "obtained" (formal "got"), "demonstrate" (formal "show"), "inquire" (formal "ask"). The register cumulates into legal-formal tone.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The meeting will commence at ten."** — formal "start"
2. **"Please inquire at reception."** — formal "ask"
3. **"We need to ascertain the cause."** — formal "find out"
4. **"The report will demonstrate the findings."** — formal "show"
5. **"The launch was postponed indefinitely."** — formal "put off"

## Unit A03 — Pass away, between jobs

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Euphemism (passed away, between jobs, let go, downsized, pre-owned, in a better place, restroom) — softening sensitive topics
> **Markers**: [denso] [euphemism]
> **Real-life context**: You handle death, job loss, age, illness, and other delicate topics with the words convention expects.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "Her grandfather _____ last spring."
   **Options**: passed away / died down / fell out / went out
   **Answer**: passed away
   **Explanation**: "Pass away" — standard euphemism for "die", used in respectful and condolence contexts. "Die" is direct and not wrong, but "passed away" is preferred in conversation, obituaries, and sensitive moments. Other euphemisms: "passed on", "is no longer with us", "lost (their) battle with...".

2. **multiple_choice** — "He's been _____ for a few months now."
   **Options**: between jobs / between works / under jobs / off jobs
   **Answer**: between jobs
   **Explanation**: "Between jobs" — euphemism for "unemployed". Implies a transition rather than a state. Used in polite conversation about employment gaps. Direct "unemployed" is fine but carries more stigma; the euphemism normalises the situation.

3. **fill_gap** — "Three hundred employees were _____ in the restructuring."
   **Hint**: Corporate euphemism for "fired"
   **Answer**: let go (or "laid off")
   **Explanation**: "Let go" — corporate euphemism for "fired". "Laid off" specifically implies layoffs (no fault of the worker). "Fired" implies cause/fault. C1 register knows when each fits. Press releases prefer "let go" or "laid off"; HR conversations may use both depending on circumstance.

4. **fix_error** — Funeral condolence: "Sorry about your grandmother — she died Tuesday, right?"
   **Hint**: Use "passed away" in condolence context
   **Answer**: Sorry about your grandmother — she passed away Tuesday, right?
   **Explanation**: "Died" in a direct condolence reads as blunt. Even when factually identical, "passed away" honours the social moment. Register-awareness is part of C1 fluency, not just vocabulary range.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The obituary was full of soft phrases. 'After a long illness, she passed away peacefully at home, surrounded by loved ones. She is survived by her husband and three children.' Compare that to the medical report: 'Cause of death: metastatic breast cancer.' Same event, two registers — one private, one public."
   **Question**: How does the medical report differ in register from the obituary?
   **Answer**: it's direct/clinical (or: it doesn't use euphemism / it states cause without softening)
   **Explanation**: The obituary uses three euphemism layers: "passed away" (died), "peacefully" (comfort claim), "is survived by" (formal family-listing phrase). The medical record uses denotative direct language. Register sits between speaker, audience, and purpose.

## Unit A04 — Not bad, no small feat

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Litotes (understatement through negation) — "not bad", "no small feat", "I wouldn't say no", "not exactly cheap"
> **Markers**: [denso] [understatement]
> **Real-life context**: You signal positive evaluation by negating a negative — adding a dry, often British-tinged sophistication.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Asked how dinner was: "_____."
   **Options**: Not bad / Bad not / Not the bad / Not so badly
   **Answer**: Not bad
   **Explanation**: "Not bad" — litotes for "actually quite good". Fixed phrase. The understatement is the praise — saying it wasn't bad is a sly compliment, especially in British English. Direct equivalent: "really good", but "not bad" carries dryness.

2. **multiple_choice** — "Translating that paper from Mandarin in three days was _____."
   **Options**: no small feat / no big deal / no small thing / not a small
   **Answer**: no small feat
   **Explanation**: "No small feat" — litotes meaning "a significant accomplishment". Fixed phrase using "no" + "small" + "feat". Synonyms: "no mean achievement", "no small thing". Used to praise without gushing.

3. **fill_gap** — Offered cake: "Well, _____."
   **Hint**: Litotes meaning "yes, please"
   **Answer**: I wouldn't say no
   **Explanation**: "I wouldn't say no" — litotes for accepting eagerly. Polite-British register. Saying "I wouldn't say no" to cake is much warmer than "yes". Cultural code: the litotes signals you want it but won't be greedy about asking.

4. **fix_error** — "The new apartment isn't cheap exactly."
   **Hint**: Word order — "not exactly cheap"
   **Answer**: The new apartment isn't exactly cheap. (or: The new apartment is not exactly cheap.)
   **Explanation**: "Not exactly + adjective" — litotes meaning "actually quite the opposite". "Not exactly cheap" means expensive. Fixed word order: "not exactly + X". "Not cheap exactly" is awkward — adverb placement matters.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "His review was a small masterclass in understatement. 'The restaurant is not without its charms. The wine list is no small achievement.    **Question**: How does the reviewer feel about returning to the restaurant?

## Unit A05 — Code-switching

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Code-switching across registers within a conversation — formal to casual, casual to formal, technical to plain — and recognising when each fits
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You move between contexts in one day — formal email, casual chat with colleague, technical discussion with peers — choosing register fluidly.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Casual chat with a friend: "How's it going?"
   **Options**: Pretty good, you? / Quite well, thank you for asking. / Things are progressing favourably. / I am content with my current trajectory.
   **Answer**: Pretty good, you?
   **Explanation**: Casual contexts demand casual answers. The other three options are register-mismatched: too formal for the friend-greeting. "Pretty good, you?" matches the casual prompt. Register-mismatch reads as sarcastic, robotic, or socially clumsy — even when grammatically perfect.

2. **multiple_choice** — Formal cover letter: "_____ in this role would allow me to grow."
   **Options**: A position / A gig / A spot / A slot
   **Answer**: A position
   **Explanation**: "Position" is the formal register noun for a job role. "Gig" is informal-casual ("a freelance gig"). "Spot" and "slot" are informal. Cover letters use "position" or "role". The register of the noun matches the rest of the letter's formality.

3. **fill_gap** — Tech meeting among engineers: "We need to _____ the deployment script before merging."
   **Hint**: Verb that fits engineer-casual register
   **Answer**: refactor (or "rewrite", "clean up")
   **Explanation**: Engineer-register prefers technical verbs in casual contexts. "Refactor" is technical but conversational. "Restructure" would be formal-corporate. "Fix" would be too vague. C1 register fluency includes knowing the verb each subculture defaults to.

4. **fix_error** — Slack message to your boss: "Yo, can you pop into the call?"
   **Hint**: "Yo" is too casual for any boss except in very informal cultures
   **Answer**: Hey, do you have a moment to join the call? (or: Could you join the call?)
   **Explanation**: "Yo" is youth-register, fits only specific subcultures. Most professional contexts read "yo" as too casual or jarring. Even in informal Slack cultures, "hey" is safer. Register-reading is part of professional C1.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Her day was a register-switching exercise. To the board, she wrote: 'I would respectfully propose we revisit the original timeline.'    **Question**: How does the speaker phrase the same underlying message to her therapist?
   **Answer**: "I think I've been moving too fast and pretending it's fine"
   **Explanation**: Three registers for one underlying idea (the timeline is wrong): formal proposal-speak to the board, casual urgency to the co-founder, vulnerable plain-speech to the therapist. C1 fluency is knowing which version fits which room.

## Unit A06 — UK vs US + Falsos cognatos cross-link

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Awareness of UK vs US English (vocabulary, spelling, gotten/got) + final reinforcement of false cognates for Lusophones (Fase B extra unit)
> **Markers**: [awareness]
> **Real-life context**: You recognise British vs American English differences and avoid the most persistent false-cognate traps that catch Portuguese-speakers even at C1.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — British English equivalent of American "elevator":
   **Options**: lift / staircase / ladder / escalator
   **Answer**: lift
   **Explanation**: "Lift" (British) = "elevator" (American). Key vocabulary pairs to know: lift/elevator, lorry/truck, flat/apartment, queue/line, pavement/sidewalk, holiday/vacation. Both forms are widely understood, but the convention shifts by region.

2. **multiple_choice** — American spelling of "colour":
   **Options**: color / colur / colore / colours
   **Answer**: color
   **Explanation**: American English drops the "u" in "-our" endings: colour→color, favour→favor, neighbour→neighbor, honour→honor. Also "-re" → "-er": centre→center, theatre→theater. Choose one convention and stay consistent in a document.

3. **fill_gap** — In American English, the past participle of "get" is often "_____" rather than British "got".
   **Hint**: alternative past participle form
   **Answer**: gotten
   **Explanation**: "Gotten" — American past participle of "get". British English uses "got" for past participle. "I've gotten better at this" (American) vs "I've got better at this" (British). Note: "have got" meaning "have/possess" uses "got" in both.

4. **fix_error** — Por português falar 'pretender', você diz em inglês: "I pretend to leave tomorrow."
   **Hint**: false cognate — "pretend" doesn't mean "intend"
   **Answer**: I intend to leave tomorrow. (or: I plan to leave tomorrow.)
   **Explanation**: "Pretend" in English = "fingir" (to feign). "Pretender" in Portuguese = "to intend" in English. Classic false cognate. Other traps: "push" (empurrar) ≠ "puxar"; "library" (biblioteca) ≠ "livraria" (bookstore); "actually" (na verdade) ≠ "atualmente" (currently).

   **Explanation**: The passage models British vocabulary (holiday/flat/lift/pavement/queue) and demonstrates correct usage of common false-cognate traps (intend, actually, realise). False cognates remain a C1 challenge — careful awareness prevents fossilised errors.

## Cross-unit consolidation

By the end of M11, the student should:
- Distinguish positively-loaded, neutral, and negatively-loaded synonyms (assertive/aggressive/pushy)
- Swap between formal and informal register pairs (commence/start, ascertain/find out, demonstrate/show)
- Use standard euphemisms for sensitive topics (passed away, between jobs, let go, pre-owned)
- Praise through litotes (not bad, no small feat, not exactly cheap, I wouldn't say no)
- Switch register fluidly across audiences in the same day
- Recognise register-mismatch as a fluency error, not just a stylistic preference
- Pick words for their emotional weight, not just their dictionary meaning
- (A06 UK/US): Recognise UK/US vocabulary pairs (lift/elevator, flat/apartment, holiday/vacation, lorry/truck, queue/line, pavement/sidewalk), spelling (colour/color, centre/center, organise/organize), and grammar (gotten/got past participle). Avoid persistent Lusophone false cognates (pretend ≠ pretender, push ≠ puxar, library ≠ livraria, actually ≠ atualmente, realise ≠ realizar, parents ≠ parentes, college ≠ colégio, eventually ≠ eventualmente, sensible ≠ sensível, intend ≠ entender, attend ≠ atender, assist ≠ assistir, exit ≠ êxito).

**End of C1 (Advanced M01–M11).** The student has worked through the structural backbone (conditionals, inversion, clefts, subjunctive, passive, participials, reporting), the diplomatic layer (hedging, idioms, discourse markers), and now connotation/register. C2 (M12–M22) builds on this for academic writing, persuasion, literary register, masterclass phrasal verbs, word formation, ellipsis, extended cohesion, modal subtlety, native pronunciation, native-like speech, and final synthesis.
