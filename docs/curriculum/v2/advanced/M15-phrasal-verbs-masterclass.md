## Unit A01 — Bring about, bring round, come round

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: "Bring" and "come" multi-meaning phrasal verbs (bring about = cause; bring round = revive/persuade; come round = visit, change mind, regain consciousness) | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: [denso] [phrasal]
> **Real-life context**: You handle one of English's most polysemous phrasal verb families — the same verb shifts meaning sharply with the particle and context.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "The reform was _____ by years of campaigning."
   **Options**: brought about / brought round / brought up / brought over
   **Answer**: brought about
   **Explanation**: "Bring about" = cause, produce (a change). Used for outcomes, results, transformations. Common register: news, history, policy ("brought about by years of pressure"). Not interchangeable with "bring round" (very different meaning).

2. **multiple_choice** — "It took an hour to _____ him after he fainted."
   **Options**: bring round / bring about / bring up / bring on
   **Answer**: bring round
   **Explanation**: "Bring round" = revive someone from unconsciousness; or convince/persuade. Two distinct meanings: medical (revive) and persuasive (convince). Context disambiguates. Note: "round" is the British spelling; American often uses "around" but both work.

3. **fill_gap** — "Why don't you _____ for dinner this weekend?"
   **Hint**: "Come round" (visit)
   **Answer**: come round
   **Explanation**: "Come round" = visit casually (British). Also: "come round to + idea" = gradually accept. "He came round to my point of view." Multiple meanings: drop by, regain consciousness, change mind. Context-dependent.

4. **fix_error** — "She brought up the change in policy through her advocacy work."
   **Hint**: For "cause", use "brought about", not "brought up"
   **Answer**: She brought about the change in policy through her advocacy work.
   **Explanation**: "Bring up" = mention, raise (a topic), or raise (children). It doesn't mean cause. For "cause" use "bring about". This kind of phrasal verb confusion is a classic C2 distinction — same verb, different particles, different meanings.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The decade had brought about changes nobody could have foreseen. Old colleagues he hadn't seen in years would come round unannounced, sit in his kitchen, and tell him things they hadn't told their own families. It took a long time to bring some of them round to the idea that he was no longer the same person they had once known."
   **Question**: What three meanings of "bring/come round" appear in the passage?
   **Answer**: 'brought about' = caused (changes); 'come round' = visit unannounced; 'bring round' = persuade/convince
   **Explanation**: Three phrasal verbs with shared form but distinct meanings: "brought about" (caused), "come round" (visit), "bring some of them round" (persuade). C2 readers track which meaning fits which context fluidly.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The protests brought about real change."** — bring about = cause
2. **"It took ten minutes to bring her round."** — bring round = revive
3. **"He came round to my idea eventually."** — come round = change mind
4. **"Why don't you come round on Saturday?"** — come round = visit
5. **"I'll bring her round to the proposal."** — bring round = persuade

## Unit A02 — Gloss over, crop up, single out

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Phrasal verbs of attention and emphasis (gloss over = downplay; crop up = appear; single out = highlight individually; bring up = mention) | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: [denso] [phrasal]
> **Real-life context**: You discuss attention — what was ignored, what surfaced, what was specifically picked out.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "The report _____ the most concerning findings."
   **Options**: glossed over / glossed up / glossed about / glossed in
   **Answer**: glossed over
   **Explanation**: "Gloss over" = pass over briefly, ignore the difficulty of, downplay. Used when someone gives shallow attention to something that deserves more. Common critical register: "The article glossed over the methodology."

2. **multiple_choice** — "Questions about the timeline _____ in every meeting."
   **Options**: cropped up / cropped out / cropped over / cropped in
   **Answer**: cropped up
   **Explanation**: "Crop up" = arise, appear unexpectedly. Inseparable. Often used for issues or topics that keep recurring. "Problems keep cropping up", "Names that crop up", "Questions cropped up". Slightly informal-conversational.

3. **fill_gap** — "She was _____ for the most demanding role on the project."
   **Hint**: "single + ___"
   **Answer**: singled out
   **Explanation**: "Single out" = pick out one person/thing from a group for special attention (positive or negative). "Single out for praise", "single out for blame". Note: separable — "singled her out" is also correct.

4. **fix_error** — "The investigator glossed over the inconsistencies and singled out them in the report."
   **Hint**: With "singled out + pronoun", the pronoun goes between
   **Answer**: The investigator glossed over the inconsistencies and singled them out in the report.
   **Explanation**: "Single out" is separable: "single them out", not "single out them". Pronouns split the phrasal verb. The other phrasal ("gloss over") is non-separable: "glossed over the inconsistencies", not "glossed the inconsistencies over". Each phrasal has its own separation rules.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The investigative report did the work the original news coverage had not. It refused to gloss over the systemic failures. It traced the small incidents that had cropped up over fifteen years, none of them sufficient on their own to draw attention. And it singled out three executives who had received warnings about each one — and chosen not to act."
   **Question**: What did the report do that the original coverage had not?
   **Answer**: refused to gloss over the systemic failures
   **Accepts**: traced the small incidents that had cropped up over fifteen years and singled out three executives
   **Explanation**: Three attention-related phrasal verbs: "gloss over" (downplay), "crop up" (appear over time), "single out" (highlight). Investigative-prose register; common in journalism and accountability writing.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The article glossed over the cost."** — downplaying
2. **"Issues keep cropping up."** — recurring problems
3. **"She was singled out for praise."** — positive highlight
4. **"He was singled out for blame."** — negative highlight
5. **"The same names crop up again and again."** — recurring appearance

## Unit A03 — Live up to, fall through, iron out

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Phrasal verbs of expectation and resolution (live up to = meet expectations; fall through = collapse; iron out = resolve; tide over = sustain temporarily) | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: [denso] [phrasal]
> **Real-life context**: You discuss expectations, failed plans, and resolved problems with phrasals that are everyday in C2 conversation.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "The sequel didn't quite _____ the original."
   **Options**: live up to / live for / live by / live with
   **Answer**: live up to
   **Explanation**: "Live up to" = meet the standard or expectation set by something/someone. "Live up to one's reputation / promises / the hype". Three-part phrasal (live + up + to). Always followed by the standard being measured against.

2. **multiple_choice** — "The deal _____ at the last minute."
   **Options**: fell through / fell over / fell down / fell out
   **Answer**: fell through
   **Explanation**: "Fall through" = collapse, fail to happen (plans, deals, agreements). The image: something falling through a gap, never reaching completion. "The arrangement fell through" = it didn't happen as planned.

3. **fill_gap** — "Let's _____ the last few disagreements before we sign."
   **Hint**: "Iron + out" — resolve
   **Answer**: iron out
   **Explanation**: "Iron out" = smooth out, resolve (problems, disagreements, wrinkles). The image: ironing a wrinkled shirt smooth. Separable phrasal — "iron them out" works too. Common in business negotiation.

4. **fix_error** — "We need to live up the customer's expectations."
   **Hint**: Three parts — "live UP TO"
   **Answer**: We need to live up to the customer's expectations.
   **Explanation**: "Live up to" is a three-part phrasal verb. Dropping "to" breaks it. The structure is: subject + live + up + to + object (the standard). Common error to drop the second particle.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The launch had not gone as planned. Two key contracts fell through in the same week. The product, despite the hype, failed to live up to expectations on the most-watched metric. Over the next quarter, the team ironed out the technical issues and rebuilt slowly. A small bridge loan from one of the founders' families tided them over until revenue caught up."
   **Question**: What helped the team financially during the difficult quarter?
   **Answer**: a small bridge loan from one of the founders' families
   **Accepts**: the bridge loan tided them over
   **Explanation**: Four C2 phrasal verbs: "fell through" (collapsed), "live up to" (meet expectations), "ironed out" (resolved), "tided them over" (sustained temporarily). Business-narrative register.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"The sequel didn't live up to the original."** — meet expectation
2. **"The deal fell through at the last minute."** — plans collapsed
3. **"Let's iron out the details."** — resolve issues
4. **"A loan tided us over for three months."** — temporary sustain
5. **"He's hard to live up to."** — high standard

## Unit A04 — Talk down to, wear down, shake off

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Phrasal verbs of interpersonal force (talk down to = patronise; wear down = gradually weaken; shake off = recover from; bear out = confirm) | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: [denso] [phrasal]
> **Real-life context**: You describe interpersonal dynamics, persistence, and recovery using common-yet-tricky phrasals.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "I hate the way she _____ to interns."
   **Options**: talks down / talks down to / talks low to / talks below
   **Answer**: talks down to
   **Explanation**: "Talk down to" = address someone as if they are inferior or less intelligent. Three-part phrasal: talk + down + to + person. Strongly negative connotation. The key C2 distinction: "talk down" (different — means convince out of something dangerous) vs "talk down to" (patronise).

2. **multiple_choice** — "Years of pressure had _____ him."
   **Options**: worn down / worn off / worn out / worn through
   **Answer**: worn down
   **Explanation**: "Wear down" = gradually weaken, exhaust through persistence. "Wear out" = exhaust through use (clothes, people physically). "Wear off" = fade away (effects, paint, novelty). Three similar phrasals, distinct meanings.

3. **fill_gap** — "She couldn't quite _____ the feeling that something was wrong."
   **Hint**: "Shake + off"
   **Answer**: shake off
   **Explanation**: "Shake off" = recover from, get rid of (a feeling, illness, pursuer). Separable: "shake the feeling off" also works. Common with abstract objects: doubt, dread, fear, a cold.

4. **fix_error** — "He worn me down with constant complaints."
   **Hint**: Tense — past simple "wore"
   **Answer**: He wore me down with constant complaints.
   **Explanation**: "Wear" is irregular: wear / wore / worn. Past simple = "wore", not "worn". Past participle = "worn" (as in "has worn me down"). C2 awareness includes irregular conjugation in phrasal verbs.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "She had learned, after years of dealing with him, what to ignore and what to absorb. He talked down to junior colleagues without realising he was doing it. He wore down even his closest collaborators through sheer repetition. And whatever doubts he had about himself, he had long since learned to shake off before they reached the surface. The new evidence, however, bore out exactly what his rivals had been saying for years."
   **Question**: What did the new evidence do?
   **Answer**: bore out (confirmed) what his rivals had been saying
   **Explanation**: Four interpersonal phrasal verbs: "talked down to" (patronised), "wore down" (exhausted through persistence), "shake off" (suppress feelings), "bore out" (confirmed). C2 narrative register.

## Unit A05 — Stem from, amount to, water down

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Phrasal verbs of origin, equivalence, and weakening (stem from = originate in; amount to = equal/total; water down = weaken; pass off as = pretend X is Y) | **Tense**: CHUNKS
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You analyse origins, quantify effects, and describe how strong things become weak.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "The problem _____ from a misalignment between teams."
   **Options**: stems / stems from / stems out / stems away
   **Answer**: stems from
   **Explanation**: "Stem from + noun" = originate in, have its source in. Used for problems, attitudes, beliefs, traditions. Botanical metaphor: stem comes from root. Common academic and analytical register.

2. **multiple_choice** — "All the talk _____ very little in the end."
   **Options**: amounted to / amounted in / amounted of / amounted for
   **Answer**: amounted to
   **Explanation**: "Amount to + noun" = equal, total, come to. Can be literal ("amounts to $100") or evaluative ("amounts to nothing"). Often used dismissively when an effort produces little.

3. **fill_gap** — "The proposal had been _____ in committee."
   **Hint**: "Water + ___"
   **Answer**: watered down
   **Explanation**: "Water down" = weaken, dilute, reduce strength of (a proposal, a drink, an idea). Often used negatively when something significant becomes insipid through compromise. Image: adding water to dilute strong drink.

4. **fix_error** — "Her success stems back from her early training."
   **Hint**: "Stem from" not "stem back from"
   **Answer**: Her success stems from her early training.
   **Explanation**: "Stem from" is the fixed phrasal — no "back" needed. "Stems back" might feel intuitive (it suggests historical roots) but it's not standard. The phrasal already implies origin.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The problem, the consultant concluded, stemmed from a culture that had never been willing to disagree publicly. Decisions were watered down before they reached the room where they would be made. By the end, what amounted to genuine policy was being passed off as bold reform, when in reality it was just consensus dressed up in unfamiliar language."
   **Question**: According to the consultant, what was being passed off as bold reform?
   **Answer**: consensus dressed up in unfamiliar language
   **Explanation**: Four C2 phrasal verbs: "stemmed from" (originated in), "watered down" (weakened), "amounted to" (equalled), "passed off as" (pretended to be). Diagnostic-prose register.
