# Module M19 — Modal subtlety

> **Level**: Advanced (C2)
> **Block**: C2 Block
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Fine shades of modal meaning — certainty (must/will/should/might/may), obligation vs inference, layered modals (might have to), quasi-modals (be supposed to / be bound to / be likely to), formal modals (ought to / shall / would)
> **Module goal**: Student controls the exact shade of certainty, obligation, willingness, or possibility — choosing modals deliberately rather than defaulting.
> **Connects to**: M20 (Pronúncia nativa) — moves from semantic nuance to phonological nuance.

## Module chunks introduced (~28)

- must (strong inference + strong obligation)
- will (high certainty + habitual)
- should (mild obligation + expected outcome)
- might / may (possibility, may slightly more formal)
- could (capability + possibility)
- might well / may well (hedged probable)
- could well (hedged probable)
- ought to (formal moral obligation)
- shall (legal/formal future)
- would (habitual past, willingness, hypothetical)
- be supposed to / be meant to (expected by convention)
- be bound to (high probability — likely outcome)
- be likely to / be unlikely to (probability statement)
- I dare say / I would venture (formal hedged opinion)
- can't have / must have / might have + past participle (already in M14 modal perfects, here refined)

## Unit A01 — Must, will, should — three shades of certainty

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Distinguishing "must" (strong inference), "will" (high certainty), and "should" (expected outcome) in present-time predictions | **Tense**: MODAL
> **Markers**: [denso] [modal]
> **Real-life context**: You predict or infer with the right strength — neither overclaiming nor underclaiming.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "He hasn't answered his phone all day. He _____ be travelling."
   **Options**: must / will / should / can
   **Answer**: must
   **Explanation**: "Must" — strong inference from evidence. "He hasn't / has not answered" → conclusion: "he must be travelling". "Will" would be slightly weaker (logical expectation), "should" weaker still (expected but uncertain), "can" doesn't fit here. "Must" matches the strength of the evidence-driven conclusion.

2. **multiple_choice** — "That _____ be the postman at the door."
   **Options**: will / must / should / can
   **Answer**: will
   **Accepts**: must
   **Explanation**: "Will" — high-certainty prediction based on routine ("It's / It is the time he usually comes"). "Must" works too if there's stronger evidence (a specific knock). The English habit of using "will" for confident predictions about the present surprises learners. "That'll be him" is everyday English.

3. **fill_gap** — "She _____ be home by now — her shift ended at six."
   **Hint**: Modal of expected outcome based on schedule
   **Answer**: should
   **Explanation**: "Should" — mild expectation based on knowledge of routine. Weaker than "must" or "will". Implies "based on what I know, this is expected — but I'm / I am not certain". Common for time-based expectations.

4. **fix_error** — "He hasn't replied to the email. He must has been busy."
   **Hint**: After modals (must/should/will), use base form — not "has been"
   **Answer**: He hasn't replied to the email. He must have been busy.
   **Explanation**: After modal "must", use base form. Past inference: "must HAVE + past participle" (must have been busy). "Must has been" mixes the modal with finite verb — ungrammatical. Common L2 error to mistime the auxiliary.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The radio silence had lasted three days. He must be off the grid — there was no other explanation. Or he might have lost his phone again, which would not be the first time. He should be back in range by Friday, but the team had stopped relying on his promises long ago."
   **Question**: According to the passage, what are the three possible explanations for his silence?
   **Answer**: he's off the grid (must), he lost his phone (might have), he should be back by Friday
   **Explanation**: Three modal shades calibrating certainty: "must be off the grid" (strong inference), "might have lost his phone" (weaker possibility), "should be back by Friday" (expected but uncertain). Each modal does specific epistemic work.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"He must be travelling."** — strong inference
2. **"That'll be the postman."** — confident prediction
3. **"She should be home by now."** — expected outcome
4. **"He must have been busy."** — past inference
5. **"That can't be right."** — strong negative inference

## Unit A02 — Might well, could well, may well

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Hedged probable — adding "well" to a modal (might well, could well, may well) for "quite possibly / quite likely" | **Tense**: MODAL
> **Markers**: [denso] [modal]
> **Real-life context**: You signal that a possibility is more than just possible — it's quite likely — without claiming certainty.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "She _____ be promoted next quarter — every signal points that way."
   **Options**: might well / might / could / would
   **Answer**: might well
   **Explanation**: "Might well" — adds force to "might". Means "quite possibly" or "very probably". The "well" pushes "might" closer to "probably". Used when evidence points strongly but you're not certain. Subtle C2 hedge.

2. **multiple_choice** — "That _____ be the explanation, but we should verify before committing."
   **Options**: could well / could / can / will
   **Answer**: could well
   **Explanation**: "Could well" — hedged but probable. Similar to "might well". The "well" elevates "could" from "is possible" to "quite plausibly". Common in analytical and academic prose.

3. **fill_gap** — "We _____ be wrong about this — but the data so far supports our reading."
   **Hint**: "May" + well
   **Answer**: may well
   **Explanation**: "May well + base form" — hedged probability. "We may well be wrong" = "It's quite possible we're / we are wrong, even likely". Formal register equivalent of "might well". Choice between "may well" / "might well" / "could well" often comes down to register.

4. **fix_error** — "He might good be right."
   **Hint**: It's "might WELL", not "might good"
   **Answer**: He might well be right.
   **Explanation**: "Might well" is a fixed collocation — "well" (adverb), not "good" (adjective). Common L2 slip. The adverb "well" modifies the modal sense, giving it more weight. No substitution possible.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The analysts were cautious in print but emphatic in private. 'The merger may well close in the second quarter — every leak points that way. The valuation could well climb another fifteen percent before announcement. And the regulators, who have stayed quiet so far, might well intervene only after the press cycle has moved on.'"
   **Question**: How does the analyst frame each claim?
   **Answer**: each one is hedged but probable — using 'may well', 'could well', 'might well'
   **Explanation**: Three hedged-probable modals: "may well close", "could well climb", "might well intervene". Each signals likelihood without certainty. The phrase carries quiet confidence — the speaker isn't asserting, but isn't doubting either. Analyst-grade register.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"She might well be promoted next quarter."** — hedged probable
2. **"That could well be the explanation."** — analytical hedge
3. **"We may well be wrong about this."** — formal hedge
4. **"It might well rain later."** — weather hedge
5. **"He might well be the next CEO."** — political hedge

## Unit A03 — Ought to, shall, would

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Formal/archaic modals — "ought to" (moral obligation), "shall" (legal/contractual future), "would" (habitual past, willingness, conditional) | **Tense**: MODAL
> **Markers**: [denso] [modal]
> **Real-life context**: You handle formal and contractual register where everyday modals are too casual.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Moral obligation: "We _____ apologise — the mistake was ours."
   **Options**: ought to / can / will / would
   **Answer**: ought to
   **Explanation**: "Ought to" — formal moral obligation, slightly more weighted than "should". "We ought to apologise" carries a sense of duty, not just advice. Less common in casual speech but standard in formal/ethical contexts.

2. **multiple_choice** — Contractual: "The tenant _____ pay rent on the first day of each month."
   **Options**: shall / will / must / might
   **Answer**: shall
   **Explanation**: "Shall" — legal/contractual obligation. "The tenant shall pay" = legally required. "Must" works too but "shall" is the contractual convention. In ordinary speech, "shall" is mostly relegated to questions ("Shall I open the window?") and legal writing.

3. **fill_gap** — Habitual past: "When I was a child, my grandmother _____ read to me every evening."
   **Hint**: Modal for habitual past
   **Answer**: would
   **Explanation**: "Would + base" for habitual past — repeated actions in a past period. Similar to "used to" but emphasises the routine. "She would read to me every evening" = it was her habit. Note: only works for repeated actions, not for past states ("She would be tall" doesn't work for past states; use "was").

4. **fix_error** — Habitual past: "Every summer, we use to visit my aunt in Coimbra."
   **Hint**: Past tense — "used to" or "would"
   **Answer**: Every summer, we used to visit my aunt in Coimbra.
   **Accepts**: ...we would visit my aunt in Coimbra.
   **Explanation**: "Use to" needs past form "used to" (note the d). Or replace with "would" for habitual past. Common spelling error — "use to" sounds like "used to" but only "used to" is grammatical past form.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The terms of the agreement were formally worded. 'The seller shall deliver the goods within thirty days. The buyer shall pay upon receipt. Both parties ought to act in good faith. Should disputes arise, the matter would be referred to arbitration.' The dryness was deliberate — every word carried legal weight."
   **Question**: What does the agreement say about disputes?
   **Answer**: they would be referred to arbitration (if they arise)
   **Explanation**: Four formal modals: "shall deliver" (contractual obligation), "shall pay" (contractual obligation), "ought to act" (moral expectation), "Should disputes arise" (inverted conditional from M01), "would be referred" (passive conditional consequence). Legal-formal register.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"We ought to apologise."** — moral obligation
2. **"The tenant shall pay on the first."** — contractual
3. **"She would read to me every evening."** — habitual past
4. **"Shall I open the window?"** — polite offer (one of the few living "shall" uses)
5. **"He ought to have known better."** — moral past obligation

## Unit A04 — Be bound to, be likely to, be supposed to

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Quasi-modals — "be supposed to / be meant to" (expectation), "be bound to" (high probability), "be likely to / be unlikely to" (probability statement) | **Tense**: MODAL · quasi
> **Markers**: [denso] [modal]
> **Real-life context**: You express probability and expectation with structures that look adjectival but function modally.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Strong expectation: "She _____ arrive any minute — her train was on time."
   **Options**: is bound to / is supposed to / is likely to / will
   **Answer**: is bound to
   **Explanation**: "Be bound to + base" = very likely, certain to happen. Stronger than "is likely to". The "bound" carries an inevitability — "tied to this outcome". Common pattern in everyday English for confident prediction.

2. **multiple_choice** — Convention: "We _____ meet at noon, but he's running late."
   **Options**: were supposed to / are likely to / are bound to / would
   **Answer**: were supposed to
   **Explanation**: "Be supposed to + base" = expected by convention/agreement. "We were supposed to meet at noon" = that was the plan. Past form "were supposed to" used when the expectation has been broken. Synonym: "be meant to".

3. **fill_gap** — Probability: "The weather forecast says rain _____ continue through the weekend."
   **Hint**: Likelihood structure
   **Answer**: is likely to
   **Explanation**: "Is likely to + base" — probability statement. Common in forecasts, predictions, analysis. "Is unlikely to" for the negative. C2 register uses these instead of "will probably" for slightly more analytical tone.

4. **fix_error** — "He is supposed call me at three."
   **Hint**: After "supposed", you need "to + base"
   **Answer**: He is supposed to call me at three.
   **Explanation**: "Be supposed to" is fixed — the "to" is part of the structure. Without it, the sentence is ungrammatical. Some learners drop "to" because they hear "supposed" + verb without registering the infinitive marker. Always "supposed TO + base".

5. **read_answer**

## Unit A05 — Modal layering

> **Sub-CEFR**: C2 | **Grammar focus**: Stacking modals with quasi-modals — "might have to / would have to / should have to / may need to / is going to have to" — for layered necessity-in-possibility | **Tense**: MODAL
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You express situations where one modal alone is not enough — necessity that depends on possibility, obligation in hypothetical contexts.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — Layered possibility + necessity: "If sales fall further, we _____ cut the budget."
   **Options**: might have to / will must / can have to / would can
   **Answer**: might have to
   **Explanation**: English doesn't allow two true modals together (e.g., "might must"). Instead, layer with quasi-modal: "might have to + base" (possibility + necessity). "Might have to cut" = "it's possible we'll / we will need to cut". Standard C2 layered modality.

2. **multiple_choice** — Hypothetical obligation: "If the proposal passes, we _____ restructure the team."
   **Options**: would have to / will have to / would must / will must
   **Answer**: would have to
   **Explanation**: "Would have to + base" — conditional obligation. "If X happens, we would have to do Y" = under that condition, the necessity would arise. Common in scenario planning and conditional analysis.

3. **fill_gap** — Anticipated obligation: "Given the new regulations, we _____ adjust our reporting process by next quarter."
   **Hint**: "Are going to" + "have to"
   **Answer**: are going to have to
   **Explanation**: "Be going to have to + base" — anticipated forced obligation. Combines "going to" (future intention/anticipation) with "have to" (necessity). Conveys that the obligation is approaching whether we like it or not. Slightly weary tone.

4. **fix_error** — "She might must work overtime this week."
   **Hint**: Two modals can't stack — use a quasi-modal
   **Answer**: She might have to work overtime this week.
   **Explanation**: "Might must" is ungrammatical — English doesn't permit two true modals together. Replace one with a quasi-modal: "might have to" or "may need to". This is one of the most common L2 errors for Spanish/Portuguese speakers, whose languages allow modal-modal sequences.

## Cross-unit consolidation

- Calibrate present-time predictions: "must" (strong inference), "will" (high certainty), "should" (expected outcome), "might/may/could" (possibility)
- Hedge probable outcomes with "might well / could well / may well"
- Use formal modals: "ought to" (moral obligation), "shall" (legal/contractual), "would" (habitual past)
- Deploy quasi-modals: "be supposed to" (convention), "be bound to" (high probability), "be likely to / unlikely to" (analytical probability), "be meant to" (soft expectation)
- Layer modals via quasi-modal pivots: "might have to / would have to / are going to have to / may need to"
- Avoid stacking two true modals together ("might must" is ungrammatical — use "might have to")
- Read contracts and analytical prose for the modal weight each verb carries
