# Module M08 — Diplomacy and hedging

> **Level**: Advanced (C1)
> **Block**: C1 Block
> **Units**: 5 (A01–A05)
> **Theme**: Modal hedging, softening for delicate communication, vague language, distancing devices, polite disagreement
> **Module goal**: Student delivers difficult messages, disagreements, and requests with appropriate softening — protecting relationships while staying clear.
> **Connects to**: M09 (Idiomas e linguagem figurada) — pivots from formal hedging to figurative texture.

## Module chunks introduced (~25)

- I would suggest...
- It might be worth + -ing
- I was wondering if...
- I'd be inclined to + base
- Could I just check...
- Wouldn't it be better to + base
- Have you considered + -ing

- With respect, I'd see it differently
- It could be argued that...
- One might say...
- In a sense...
- Sort of / kind of (vague softeners)
- Roughly / approximately / more or less

## Unit A01 — I was wondering if

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Past continuous softening (I was wondering if / I was hoping...) for polite requests | **Tense**: HEDGING
> **Markers**: [denso] [hedging]
> **Real-life context**: You ask for favours, time, or attention with maximum politeness.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "_____ if I could have a few minutes of your time."
   **Options**: I was wondering / I wondered / I am wondering / Wonder
   **Answer**: I was wondering
   **Explanation**: "I was wondering if + clause" — past continuous as hedge. Despite the past form, the request is fully present/future. Past continuous distances the request, softening it. "I wonder" feels blunter; "I am wondering" presumes immediacy.

2. **multiple_choice** — "_____ you might be able to help with something."
   **Options**: I was hoping / I hoped / I am hoping / I hope
   **Answer**: I was hoping
   **Explanation**: "I was hoping that..." — past continuous of hope, used to introduce a soft request. Equivalent to "I'm / I am wondering" but with slight emotional warmth. "I hope" reads as already-committed; the past form softens.

3. **fill_gap** — "_____ if you'd mind looking over this draft when you have a moment."
   **Hint**: "I was wondering"
   **Answer**: I was wondering
   **Explanation**: Soft request opener. The full structure: "I was wondering if you'd / you would mind + -ing". Double softening: past continuous + "would you mind" + -ing.

4. **fix_error** — "I want you to look at this report."
   **Hint**: Soften with "I was wondering if you could..."
   **Answer**: I was wondering if you could take a look at this report.
   **Accepts**: I was hoping you might look at this report.
   **Explanation**: "I want you to" is direct and can read as demanding. The softer C1 register uses "I was wondering if you could..." or similar hedged forms. Especially important when asking up the hierarchy.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The email began the way the senior partner's emails always did. 'I was wondering if you'd have a moment this afternoon. There's a small matter I was hoping to discuss in person — nothing urgent, but it would be helpful to talk it through.' She had learned that 'nothing urgent' from him often meant exactly the opposite."
   **Question**: How did the senior partner soften the request?
   **Answer**: with "I was wondering if you'd have a moment" + "I was hoping to discuss"
   **Explanation**: Two past-continuous hedges layered ("I was wondering... I was hoping..."), plus "small matter", "nothing urgent", and "it would be helpful". Diplomatic register building room for both parties.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"I was wondering if you'd have a moment."** — softened time request
2. **"I was hoping we could discuss the budget."** — softened topic introduction
3. **"I was wondering if you might consider it."** — softened proposal
4. **"I was hoping for your advice on this."** — softened ask
5. **"I was wondering if there's any flexibility."** — softened negotiation opener

## Unit A02 — It might be worth

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Hedging suggestions with modals (it might be worth + -ing, you might want to + base, you may wish to + base, perhaps + clause) | **Tense**: HEDGING
> **Markers**: [denso] [hedging]
> **Real-life context**: You suggest ideas without imposing — leaving room for the other to consider, accept, or decline.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "_____ checking the figures one more time before we send."
   **Options**: It might be worth / It is worth / It is worthy of / It worth
   **Answer**: It might be worth
   **Explanation**: "It might be worth + -ing" — softened suggestion. "Might" hedges the assertion; "be worth + -ing" attaches the action. Common business-polite formula. "It is worth" sounds definite; the hedge "might be" leaves room.

2. **multiple_choice** — "_____ to talk to her directly."
   **Options**: You might want / You wanted / You can / You will
   **Answer**: You might want
   **Explanation**: "You might want to + base" — friendly softened suggestion. Avoids the command implicit in "You should..." or "You need to...". Common in mentor-style speech.

3. **fill_gap** — "_____ wish to revisit this decision later."
   **Hint**: "You may" (formal modal hedge)
   **Answer**: You may
   **Explanation**: "You may wish to + base" — formal-polite suggestion. Common in written business and academic prose. Slightly more elevated than "you might want to".

4. **fix_error** — "You should fix the introduction."
   **Hint**: Soften with hedged suggestion
   **Answer**: It might be worth revisiting the introduction.
   **Accepts**: You might want to look at the introduction again.
   **Explanation**: "You should" can land as criticism. Hedged forms preserve face — same content, different relational impact. C1 register often demands the softer version.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The editor's notes were a masterclass in tact. 'It might be worth considering whether the third paragraph adds anything new. You may wish to revisit the opening — perhaps a shorter sentence would land harder. Have you thought about cutting the final image entirely?' Every suggestion left room for the author's pride."
   **Question**: Which hedging phrase opens the editor's first suggestion?
   **Answer**: It might be worth
   **Accepts**: might be worth / might be worth considering
   **Explanation**: "It might be worth + -ing" é uma das estruturas mais diplomáticas em inglês escrito formal. Os outros hedges no trecho: "You may wish to", "perhaps", "Have you thought about" — todos preservam a autonomia do autor.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"It might be worth checking the data."** — soft suggestion
2. **"You might want to consider another angle."** — friendly hedge
3. **"You may wish to revisit this later."** — formal hedge
4. **"Perhaps a shorter title would work better."** — perhaps + clause
5. **"Have you thought about asking the team?"** — question-as-suggestion

## Unit A03 — With respect, I'd see it differently

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Polite disagreement (I take your point, but / With respect, / I'd see it differently / I'm not entirely sure) | **Tense**: HEDGING
> **Markers**: [denso] [hedging]
> **Real-life context**: You disagree firmly without damaging the relationship.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "_____, I don't think that approach will work here."
   **Options**: With respect / In respect / On respect / By respect
   **Answer**: With respect
   **Explanation**: "With respect, ..." opens polite disagreement. Often pairs with a counter-claim. Used heavily in formal meetings and diplomatic exchanges. Note: "With respect" is firmer than "With all due respect" — the latter has become slightly ironic in some contexts.

2. **multiple_choice** — "I take your point, _____ I'd see it a little differently."
   **Options**: but / and / so / however
   **Answer**: but
   **Explanation**: "I take your point, but..." — acknowledges the speaker before pivoting. "But" is the natural pivot. "However" works too but is more formal-written.

3. **fill_gap** — "_____ entirely sure I agree with that framing."
   **Hint**: "I'm not"
   **Answer**: I'm not
   **Explanation**: "I'm / I am not entirely sure I agree" — soft disagreement. "Not entirely" hedges; "I'm / I am not sure I agree" is softer than "I disagree". Common in academic and professional contexts.

4. **fix_error** — "You're wrong about that."
   **Hint**: Soften with "I'd see it differently"
   **Answer**: I'd see it a little differently.
   **Accepts**: With respect, I'd see it differently.
   **Explanation**: "You're / You are wrong" is blunt and damaging. "I'd / I would see it differently" presents the same disagreement without attacking the other person. Crucial diplomatic shift.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The diplomat had a phrase for every situation. 'With respect, I'd see the geography rather differently. I take your point about the timeline, but the local context complicates it. I'm not entirely convinced we're talking about the same problem.' Each disagreement was delivered like a gift, wrapped carefully."
   **Question**: How does the diplomat disagree without offending?
   **Answer**: hedged each disagreement ("With respect", "I take your point but", "I'm not entirely convinced")
   **Explanation**: Three diplomatic disagreement patterns layered. Each preserves the other's face while expressing genuine difference. Diplomat-grade C1.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"With respect, I'd see it differently."** — formal disagreement
2. **"I take your point, but I'm not sure."** — acknowledge + pivot
3. **"I'm not entirely convinced of that."** — soft denial
4. **"That's one way of looking at it."** — diplomatic non-agreement
5. **"I'd push back gently on that."** — informal hedge

## Unit A04 — One might say, In a sense

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Impersonal hedging (one might say, it could be argued, in a sense, to some extent, arguably) | **Tense**: HEDGING
> **Markers**: [denso] [hedging]
> **Real-life context**: You float a position without claiming it as your own — useful in academic writing, debate, careful conversation.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "_____ that the policy has done more harm than good."
   **Options**: It could be argued / It could argue / Arguable / One arguing
   **Answer**: It could be argued
   **Explanation**: "It could be argued that..." floats a position without committing the speaker. Equivalent to "Some would argue that..." or "One might say that...". Academic and debate register.

2. **multiple_choice** — "_____, the company has lost direction."
   **Options**: In a sense / In sense / On a sense / By a sense
   **Answer**: In a sense
   **Explanation**: "In a sense, ..." softens an assertion by indicating partial truth. "In some sense / in a way" similar. Hedges what would otherwise be a strong claim.

3. **fill_gap** — "_____ that there are no simple solutions here."
   **Hint**: "One might say"
   **Answer**: One might say
   **Explanation**: "One might say that..." — impersonal hedging. Common in academic writing and op-eds. Distances the speaker from the claim while still introducing it.

4. **fix_error** — "Arguably is the best film of the decade."
   **Hint**: "Arguably" needs to attach to a clause, not stand alone
   **Answer**: Arguably, it's the best film of the decade.
   **Accepts**: It's arguably the best film of the decade.
   **Explanation**: "Arguably" is an adverb — attaches to a clause. "Arguably is" treats it as a verb form, which fails. Either fronted ("Arguably, X is...") or mid-position ("It's / It is arguably the best...").

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "The reviewer hedged everywhere. 'It could be argued that this is the author's strongest work. One might say the prose has matured considerably. In a sense, the book completes a trilogy that began twenty years ago. Arguably, the ending falters — but only slightly.' Even the critique was wrapped in conditionals."
   **Question**: According to the reviewer, what arguably falters?
   **Answer**: the ending
   **Explanation**: Four hedging devices: "It could be argued", "One might say", "In a sense", "Arguably". Critic-grade C1 prose — opinions floated, not asserted.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"It could be argued that we acted too quickly."** — debate hedge
2. **"One might say the project was ambitious."** — impersonal float
3. **"In a sense, this is what we hoped for."** — partial truth
4. **"To some extent, the criticism is fair."** — partial agreement
5. **"Arguably, the best season they've had."** — adverb hedge

## Unit A05 — Sort of, more or less

> **Sub-CEFR**: C1 | **Grammar focus**: Vague language (sort of, kind of, more or less, roughly, approximately, give or take, somewhat) | **Tense**: HEDGING
> **Markers**: —
> **Real-life context**: You communicate when you don't have precise information, or when softening makes the moment kinder.

### 1. Grammar (5 exercises)

1. **multiple_choice** — "It's _____ what I expected."
   **Options**: sort of / type of / kind / similar
   **Answer**: sort of
   **Explanation**: "Sort of" / "kind of" — vague hedge, very common in spoken English. Softens claims, expresses approximation. Adverb position: before adjective ("sort of strange"), before verb ("I sort of agree"), or stand-alone.

2. **multiple_choice** — "We have _____ fifty people on the team."
   **Options**: roughly / rough / roughed / roughing
   **Answer**: roughly
   **Explanation**: "Roughly + number" — adverb signaling approximation. Synonyms: approximately, about, around, more or less. Each slightly different in register and precision.

3. **fill_gap** — "It cost ten thousand euros, _____."
   **Hint**: "give or take"
   **Answer**: give or take
   **Explanation**: "Give or take" — idiomatic phrase meaning "plus or minus a small amount". Placed at end of clause, after the figure. Casual register.

4. **fix_error** — "I'm somewhat agree with you."
   **Hint**: "Somewhat" modifies adjective/adverb, not verb directly. Use "kind of agree" or "somewhat agreeable"
   **Answer**: I kind of agree with you.
   **Accepts**: I somewhat agree with you, with the right rhythm.
   **Explanation**: "Somewhat" modifies adjectives/adverbs in formal usage ("somewhat strange"). Modern spoken English does allow "I somewhat agree", but "kind of agree" / "sort of agree" is more natural.

5. **read_answer**
   **Passage**: "Asked how the launch had gone, the founder hesitated. 'Mixed, I think. We hit roughly seventy percent of our targets, give or take. The team is sort of exhausted but more or less proud. We're somewhat behind on shipping, though — I'd say two weeks, approximately.' She had mastered the art of imprecision."
   **Question**: How behind on shipping is the team, approximately?
   **Answer**: about two weeks
   **Accepts**: two weeks, approximately
   **Explanation**: Six vague-language markers in one paragraph: "roughly", "give or take", "sort of", "more or less", "somewhat", "approximately". Spoken C1 leans heavily on these for natural rhythm.

### 2. Listening/Speaking (5 phrases)

1. **"It's sort of what I expected."** — vague approximation
2. **"We have roughly fifty employees."** — numerical hedge
3. **"It cost ten thousand, give or take."** — financial vague
4. **"I'm somewhat surprised by that."** — emotion vague
5. **"More or less, that's the plan."** — outcome hedge
